## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The jade-dew green tea — shaded to the point of transformation,
> yielding the highest L-theanine and deepest umami of any tea.

**Short description:**

> Gyokuro is a premium Japanese green tea grown in heavy shade —
> typically 70-90% light reduction — for at least 20 days before
> harvest. The shading profoundly changes the leaf chemistry:
> L-theanine accumulates dramatically (the plant normally converts
> it to catechins under UV light), caffeine increases as a stress
> response, and chlorophyll concentrates (producing the deep jade
> color that names the tea). The result is an almost paradoxical
> cup — high caffeine but deeply calm, high amino acids but minimal
> bitterness, intense umami without the seaweed-heaviness of
> matcha. Produced in very small quantities; Gyokuro represents
> less than 0.3% of Japanese tea production.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- umami
- marine
- sweet
- grassy
- creamy

**Aroma notes:**

> Distinctive and unlike any other tea. The defining quality is
> *ooika* (covered aroma) — a complex fragrance between green
> vegetables, dried seaweed, and fresh-cut grass. Dimethyl sulfide
> — the same compound present in nori seaweed — develops during
> the shading process, producing the marine note that describes
> good Gyokuro. Notes of steamed edamame, spinach, sometimes
> asparagus. The aroma rises in waves rather than asserting
> immediately.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Thick, almost soupy. "Silky" is the most common descriptor.
> First infusion at proper low temperature feels like a coating
> rather than a liquid — leaves a lingering savory film that
> persists long after the cup is empty. Minimal astringency when
> brewed correctly; this is a tea that rewards patience and
> technique over haste.

**Basic tastes:**

> - `umami` (5) — **dominant, highest in the catalog.** The
>   L-theanine + glutamic acid combination produces the deepest
>   umami of any beverage. This is why Gyokuro is sometimes served
>   in small ceremonial portions rather than as a regular hot drink.
> - `sweet` (3) — characteristic "theanine sweetness" — not like
>   sugar but a soft natural sweetness that builds on the palate.
> - `bitter` (1 at low temp / 3 at high temp) — minimal when brewed
>   at the recommended 50-60°C; jumps dramatically if brewed above
>   70°C. This is the most temperature-sensitive tea in the catalog.

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [50, 70] | traditional, Japanese Tea Association | **Far lower than any other true tea.** 50°C for first infusion is standard; 60°C for more casual brewing. Above 70°C the tea becomes bitter and loses umami character. |
| **time range (seconds)** | [60, 120] | traditional | 1-2 minutes Western-style but most premium Gyokuro is brewed with tiny water volume (5g leaf per 30-50ml water) for 1-2 minutes, yielding just a few sips of concentrated "jade essence" |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 45 | Csupor 2016; Yunomi | Actually **higher** than most green teas — shade stress elevates caffeine. Well-buffered by extreme L-theanine ratio. |
| **dose** | 5g per 30-50ml (gongfu-style) / 2.5g per 200ml (Western) | traditional | Gongfu-style is the canonical brewing; Western works but sacrifices character |

## 6. Extraction profiles — three temp anchors + time behavior

| sources | traditional (counter-example) |

### 6d. Temperature is everything

> Gyokuro is the most temperature-sensitive tea in the catalog.
> The chemistry behind this:
>
> - L-theanine extracts reasonably well at any temperature,
>   including cold water
> - Caffeine extracts faster at higher temperatures
> - Catechins (bitter) extract aggressively above ~70°C
>
> At 50°C, you get mostly L-theanine + some caffeine. At 80°C+,
> you get a much more catechin-heavy brew — which is fine for
> sencha, wrong for Gyokuro.
>
> **Cold-brew Gyokuro** is a legitimate technique — overnight cold
> brewing yields an extraordinarily umami-rich, almost broth-like
> tea. Some premium Japanese tea shops serve Gyokuro exclusively
> this way.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**Known interactions:**

> - **Caffeine (~45mg/cup)** — comparable to black tea despite
>   Gyokuro's "gentle" reputation. Shade stress elevates caffeine
>   content. Standard caffeine cautions apply. `established`
> - **L-theanine** — highest of any tea. May enhance effects of
>   relaxation-supporting medications or supplements; generally
>   considered safe but worth awareness for those taking GABA-
>   affecting drugs. `attested`
> - **Iron absorption** — tannin content is actually lower than
>   sencha or black tea due to shading, but non-zero.
>   `established`

**Dosage cautions:**

> Gyokuro's small-portion traditional brewing naturally limits
> consumption; gongfu-style Gyokuro sessions typically yield
> equivalent to 1-2 regular cups total despite multiple
> infusions. Heavier Western brewing could deliver significant
> caffeine if consumed in multiple cups.

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"Gyokuro has zero caffeine because it's so calming"** —
>   Myth. Gyokuro has MORE caffeine than sencha; the calmness
>   comes from L-theanine buffering, not caffeine absence.
>   `folk`
> - **"Shading always produces better tea"** — Gyokuro and matcha
>   are shaded because the chemistry suits their styles. Shading
>   other teas (e.g., shade-grown Darjeeling) doesn't necessarily
>   improve them — and can reduce aromatic complexity that
>   sun-grown teas develop. `attested`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origin:**

> Gyokuro is grown from *Camellia sinensis var. sinensis* cultivars
> specifically selected for high theanine accumulation under
> shade: Gokou, Okumidori, Saemidori, Asahi, Yamakai. These are
> distinct from Yabukita (the dominant Japanese sencha cultivar).
> Cultivar × shading interaction is a key variable in Gyokuro
> quality. `verified`

**Historical timeline:**

> - **Early 1800s:** Uji tea farmers begin experimenting with
>   straw-mat coverings originally intended as frost protection.
>   They notice the shaded leaves produce distinctly different
>   tea. `attested`
> - **1835 (Tenpō 6):** Yamamoto Kahei the 6th, head of the
>   Yamamotoyama tea merchant family, visits Uji to study *tencha*
>   processing (tencha = matcha's raw material). Experimenting
>   with Uji's covering method, he produces a new type of green
>   tea and names it *Tamanotsuyu* (玉の露, "Jade Dew"). This is
>   considered the birth year of Gyokuro. `verified`
> - **1841:** Eguchi Shigejuro refines Gyokuro processing using
>   the sencha production method invented by Nagatani Soen in
>   1738. `verified`
> - **Late Edo period:** Gyokuro becomes a commercial tea grade.
>   `verified`
> - **Late 19th century - early 20th century:** Shading technology
>   develops — traditional bamboo-and-rice-straw coverings (*tana*)
>   give way to synthetic shade nets (*jikagise*). Both still used
>   today for different grades. `verified`
> - **21st century:** Yame (Fukuoka) overtakes Uji in competition
>   prizes — Yame producers have held 26 of the top 30 positions
>   in the National Tea Fair Gyokuro category. Uji remains the
>   historical home and still commands premium prices. `verified`

**The shading process (*ōishita*):**

> - **Timing:** Starts when new spring shoots appear (usually
>   mid-April); lasts 20+ days through harvest.
> - **Material:** Traditional method uses straw mats on bamboo
>   scaffolding (*hon-zu*), producing the highest-grade Gyokuro.
>   Modern method uses synthetic black netting directly over
>   plants (*jikagise*). *Hon-zu* Gyokuro commands significantly
>   higher prices.
> - **Progression:** Often staged — first 70% shade, increasing to
>   80%, then 90% in final days. Higher grades receive more shade
>   for longer.
> - **Chemistry driven:** Shading prevents UV-driven conversion of
>   L-theanine to catechins. The plant produces more L-theanine as
>   the shade deepens; stress response also elevates caffeine;
>   chlorophyll accumulates (producing the deep jade color).
> `verified`

**Three major regions:**

> 1. **Uji, Kyoto (~30 minutes south of Kyoto)** — The historical
>    home. Refined, balanced, almost floral alongside the marine
>    depth. The Yamamotoyama legacy still defines Uji style. Hand-
>    picked estate Uji Gyokuro from specific producers is the
>    benchmark standard.
> 2. **Yame, Fukuoka** — The competition powerhouse. Natural
>    morning fog provides extra light diffusion ("natural Gyokuro"
>    conditions even before artificial shade). Richer, more
>    deeply savory than Uji; less floral, more mineral/broth-like.
>    Yame accounts for over 40% of Japanese Gyokuro production.
> 3. **Shizuoka (Okabe district)** — Smaller production, more
>    accessible price point. Rounder, less intensely marine — often
>    the best entry point for Gyokuro newcomers.
> `verified`

**Processing:**

> After shading and hand-harvesting (leaves are "pulled" rather
> than "picked" — stems between fingers, multiple leaves at once),
> Gyokuro processing follows the same steps as sencha:
>
> 1. Steaming (*mushi*) — 30-60 seconds to halt enzymatic oxidation
> 2. Rolling (*juuri*) — shapes leaves into characteristic needles
> 3. Drying
> 4. Sifting and blending
> 5. Final roasting (*hiire*) — very gentle for Gyokuro, to
>    preserve umami character (unlike sencha which tolerates
>    stronger final firing)
>
> The delicate final *hiire* step is what separates Gyokuro from
> sencha craftsmanship. `verified`

**Tea culture context — the meditation connection:**

> Japanese Zen monks have long favored shade-grown green teas for
> meditation support. Matcha (fully-shaded, ground-leaf) is the
> classic meditation tea; Gyokuro (shaded whole-leaf) shares much
> of the same chemistry. The L-theanine + moderate caffeine profile
> produces a state sometimes called "relaxed alertness" —
> characterized by alpha-wave brain activity increases documented
> in EEG studies (Nobre 2008 and others). The anti-stress effects
> of shade-grown tea were specifically studied by Unno et al.
> 2016, using shade-grown green tea with enriched theanine to
> prevent psychosocial-stress-induced adrenal hypertrophy in
> mice. `attested`

**TCM framing:**

> Like all unoxidized green teas, Gyokuro is TCM-cooling (Yin).
> Shade cultivation intensifies this — the tea is considered
> particularly suitable for hot weather, for people with "yin
> deficiency with heat" patterns, and for calming excited states.
> Not recommended for cold weather or cold-pattern conditions.
> `attested`

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **`focus` 5 is the new catalog maximum.** Gyokuro earns it via
   the most extreme L-theanine:caffeine profile in tea. Future
   catalog entries need to fit this calibration — if matcha is
   added, it could match (it's more-shaded than Gyokuro). Most
   other teas should stay at 3-4 focus to preserve relative
   meaning.

2. **Gyokuro is the most temperature-sensitive tea in the catalog.**
   Current brewing-parameter model treats all teas similarly —
   but for Gyokuro, the difference between 50°C and 80°C is the
   difference between the tea's signature character and a mediocre
   cup. Worth considering whether the data model should flag
   "temperature-critical" ingredients.

3. **Multi-infusion matters even more than for other teas.**
   Traditional Gyokuro brewing produces 3-5 meaningfully different
   infusions from one dose. Single-infusion data points for
   Gyokuro are especially lossy.

4. **Cultivar variation within Gyokuro is significant.** Gokou-
   based Gyokuro tastes meaningfully different from Saemidori-
   based. The Japanese tea competition circuit tracks cultivar;
   retail consumers usually don't see this information. Herbanium
   could surface it for premium shoppers if the catalog ever gets
   that granular.

5. **Matcha relationship.** Matcha is ground tencha (shade-grown
   leaf without Gyokuro's rolling). Same cultivars, same shading,
   different final processing. Matcha is not currently in
   Herbanium's catalog; would make a natural complement to
   Gyokuro if added — and would push `focus` to 5+ potentially.

6. **Hon-zu vs. jikagise shading is a quality marker not in the
   model.** Traditional straw-mat *hon-zu* shading produces
   noticeably superior Gyokuro at 3-5x the price. This is a
   sourcing-quality variable beyond what standard ingredient data
   represents.

7. **The "marine" flavor descriptor surprises some drinkers.**
   The seaweed-like note from dimethyl sulfide is actually a
   quality marker for Gyokuro, but Western-market tea education
   often treats this as a flaw. Worth flagging in user-facing
   copy so newcomers expect (and appreciate) this character.

## Addendum — prose claim audit (2026-08-23)

**The 50°C, 60 ml steep — sourced to practice.** The steep-timer content
gives gyokuro a small kyusu and about 60 ml of water at 50°C. Those
numbers are the conventional Japanese preparation and match §5's brew
grid: the point of the low temperature is to hold back catechin
extraction so the shade-grown theanine dominates, and the small volume
is what keeps the leaf-to-water ratio high enough for that to be worth
doing. Treating gyokuro like sencha — hotter water, more of it — pulls
the bitterness the shading exists to avoid. `verified`
