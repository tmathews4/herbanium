## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The Western sleep specialist — a smelly root that has been
> putting Europeans to bed since Hippocrates' time, with a
> benzodiazepine-adjacent GABA-A receptor mechanism that
> earns its place as the catalog's first `sleepy` 5 ingredient.

**Short description:**

> Valerian is the dried root of *Valeriana officinalis*, a
> tall flowering perennial plant native to Europe and parts of
> Asia, now naturalized across temperate regions globally. The
> root has been used as a sedative and sleep aid in Western
> herbal medicine for at least 2,000 years — Hippocrates
> mentions it (~400 BCE), Galen prescribed it for insomnia
> (~150 CE), and it remained one of the most-used sleep herbs
> in European medicine through the medieval period and into
> modern times. Pharmacologically, valerian's active compounds
> include valerenic acid (the most well-characterized),
> valepotriates, valerenol, and various sesquiterpenes. The
> primary mechanism is **GABA-A receptor modulation** — valerian
> binds to a different subunit (the beta subunit) than
> benzodiazepines (which bind the gamma subunit), but produces
> similar inhibitory neurotransmission effects. Additionally,
> valerian inhibits GABA transaminase (similar to lemon balm's
> mechanism, increasing brain GABA levels). The clinical
> evidence base is moderate — multiple RCTs and meta-analyses
> show benefit for insomnia, with effect sizes ranging from
> modest to comparable to short-acting benzodiazepines for some
> sleep parameters. Notably effective in combination with lemon
> balm (Cerny 1999 multicenter trial). The most distinctive
> feature is the **smell** — dried valerian root has a strong,
> funky, almost sock-like odor that surprises first-time users.
> Cats are famously attracted to it (the smell is similar to
> some pheromones); humans tend toward less enthusiasm.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- earthy
- musky
- pungent
- bitter
- woody

**Aroma notes:**

> **Distinctively unpleasant by design.** Dried valerian root
> has a strong funky-musky-cheesy odor that's been compared to
> dirty socks, old gym clothes, or aged cheese. The dominant
> aromatic compounds are isovaleric acid and various
> sesquiterpenes; isovaleric acid is the same compound that
> gives sweaty feet their characteristic smell. The Greek name
> for valerian, *phu* (φοῦ), is reportedly an exclamation of
> disgust (*"phew!"*) — a 2,400-year-old joke about the smell
> that survives in the botanical record.
>
> **Cats find it irresistible.** The smell is similar to certain
> feline pheromones; cats respond similarly to valerian as to
> catnip. Worth knowing if you have cats and store valerian.

**Mouthfeel:**

> The flavor matches the smell — strong, funky, bitter,
> astringent. Most users find plain valerian tea unpalatable.
> Traditional preparations almost always combine valerian with
> better-tasting herbs (lemon balm, chamomile, lavender) to
> mask the flavor while gaining the sleep effect. Modern
> commercial sleep tea blends frequently include valerian as
> a small fraction (10-20%) supported by stronger flavors.

**Basic tastes:**

> - `bitter` (3-4) — pronounced
> - `astringent` (3) — significant
> - `earthy` (4) — defining
> - `pungent` (3) — the funky aromatic register
> - `sweet` (0) — none

> **Flavor warning:** Valerian shares the catalog's
> "challenging-flavored ingredient" club with ashwagandha. Both
> are challenging-tasting roots that benefit from masking with
> more palatable companions. User-facing copy should set
> expectations honestly — valerian is medicinal-tasting, and
> that's part of its identity.

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [85, 95] | traditional | Slightly below boil; the volatile aromatics include some heat-sensitive compounds. Most commercial blends specify near-boiling water |
| **time range (seconds)** | [600, 900] | traditional, ref-aafp-2003 | 10-15 minutes minimum for therapeutic effect. Root material extracts slowly. AAFP equivalence: 2-3g dried herb soaked 10-15 min equals 300-600mg standardized extract |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 0 | universal | Caffeine-free |
| **dose** | 1-2 tsp dried root per 250ml; OR 2-3g for therapeutic strength | traditional, ref-aafp-2003 | Clinical trials use 300-600mg standardized extract; tea-strength delivers a meaningful but less concentrated dose |

> **Timing matters more than for any other catalog ingredient:**
>
> Valerian's sleep-promoting effect typically begins 30-60
> minutes after consumption and peaks at 1-2 hours. Best
> consumed:
> - **30-60 minutes before intended bedtime** for most users
> - **2 hours before bedtime** for users who find valerian
>   produces vivid dreams or restless sleep
> - **NOT in afternoon or with daytime activities** —
>   sedative effects are real and real
>
> Multi-dose effect (Cerny 1999 polysomnography study): single
> doses show modest sleep effects; **continuous nightly use for
> 14+ days produces stronger and more reliable sleep
> improvement.** This is unusual for sleep herbs (most work
> acutely) and worth surfacing in user-facing copy.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**Sedation warnings — explicit and prominent:**

> Valerian produces real sedation. Users must:
>
> - **NOT drive or operate machinery** within 4-6 hours of
>   therapeutic-dose valerian
> - **NOT combine with alcohol** — additive sedation can be
>   dangerous
> - **NOT combine with prescription sedatives** without medical
>   guidance — additive effects with benzodiazepines, opioids,
>   sleep medications, anti-anxiety medications
>
> User-facing copy must surface these clearly. Valerian is a
> functional sleep aid, not a casual evening tea. `established`

**Known interactions:**

> - **Benzodiazepines, opioids, sleep medications:** Strongly
>   additive sedation. Avoid combining without medical
>   guidance. `established`
> - **Alcohol:** Additive sedation; avoid combining. `established`
> - **CYP3A4 substrates:** Some pre-clinical evidence valerian
>   may modestly induce or inhibit CYP3A4; theoretical
>   interaction with many medications. `attested`
> - **Benzodiazepine withdrawal:** Interestingly, valerian may
>   help attenuate benzodiazepine withdrawal symptoms (Andreatini
>   2002). Worth noting for users in supervised tapering.
>   `attested`
> - **St. John's Wort, kava, GABAergic herbs (chamomile,
>   passionflower, lemon balm, ashwagandha):** Additive
>   sedation. Combinations may be appropriate (Cerny 1999
>   valerian-lemon balm) but should be intentional rather
>   than accidental. `attested`

**Pregnancy:**

> Limited safety data. Most clinicians recommend AVOIDING
> valerian in pregnancy and breastfeeding due to insufficient
> evidence rather than known harm. Some traditional European
> use during pregnancy exists but isn't rigorously documented.
> Conservative practice: avoid. `attested`

**Liver concerns:**

> Rare cases of liver toxicity have been reported with valerian
> supplements, primarily with high-dose long-term use or
> products contaminated with related toxic species (Heliotropium
> contamination has occurred). Tea-strength use of clean valerian
> is not associated with liver toxicity, but users with
> pre-existing liver conditions should consult healthcare
> providers. `attested`

**The "paradoxical stimulation" effect:**

> A small minority of users (estimated 5-10%) experience
> paradoxical stimulation rather than sedation from valerian —
> feeling alert, restless, or even agitated rather than sleepy.
> This is documented in the clinical literature. If it happens,
> discontinue use; valerian isn't right for everyone. `attested`

**Vivid dreams:**

> Some users report unusually vivid or intense dreams with
> valerian use, especially at therapeutic doses. Not harmful
> but worth knowing. `attested`

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"Valerian is addictive"** — No evidence of physical
>   dependence or addiction. Some users find sleep onset
>   harder after stopping continuous use, but this is mild
>   rebound rather than withdrawal. `folk`
> - **"Valerian is unsafe long-term"** — Limited long-term
>   safety data, but no specific evidence of harm. AAFP guidance
>   suggests not exceeding 4-6 weeks continuous use without
>   medical evaluation as conservative practice. `attested`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origin:**

> *Valeriana officinalis* is native to Europe (especially
> central and northern Europe) and parts of Western Asia. Now
> naturalized across temperate North America, parts of South
> America, and elsewhere. Grows as a tall (1-1.5m) perennial
> with feathery leaves and umbel-like clusters of small white
> or pink flowers (the flowers are sometimes used in
> arrangements; the medicinal value is in the root). The plant
> tolerates moist soils and partial shade; common garden
> escape in appropriate climates. Several related species
> (*V. wallichii* / *V. jatamansi* in India, *V. fauriei* in
> East Asia) are used similarly in different traditional
> medicine systems. `verified`

**Historical timeline — among the most-documented sleep herbs in Western medicine:**

> - **~400 BCE:** Hippocrates references valerian in sleep
>   contexts. The Greek name *phu* (φοῦ) reportedly an
>   exclamation of disgust at the smell — preserved in the
>   botanical record. `verified`
> - **~70 CE:** Dioscorides documents valerian in *De Materia
>   Medica* as treatment for various conditions including
>   insomnia. `verified`
> - **~150 CE:** Galen prescribes valerian for insomnia,
>   recognizing it as a primary sleep medicine. `verified`
> - **Medieval European period:** Continuous use; valerian
>   referred to as "all-heal" (Anglo-Saxon name reflecting
>   broad use beyond just sleep). The herbalist Hildegard of
>   Bingen (~1150 CE) mentions it. The English name "setwall"
>   appears in medieval texts. `verified`
> - **17th century:** John Gerard's Herbal (1597) and
>   Nicholas Culpeper's Complete Herbal (1653) both feature
>   valerian as a major sleep and nervous-system herb.
>   `verified`
> - **18th-19th century:** Valerian remains a primary
>   prescribed sleep medication in European and American
>   medicine. The first "official" pharmacopoeia listings
>   appear. Used during World War I to treat shell shock
>   (early PTSD). `verified`
> - **Mid-20th century:** Synthetic benzodiazepines (Valium
>   marketed 1963) largely displace valerian in mainstream
>   medicine, though the herbal use continues in folk
>   tradition and naturopathic medicine. `verified`
> - **1980s-1990s:** Renewed clinical research interest.
>   Mechanism research begins identifying GABA-A receptor
>   binding (1990s onward). `verified`
> - **2000s onward:** Multiple clinical trials and systematic
>   reviews. AAFP review (2003) summarizes evidence as
>   moderate. Cerny 1999 multicenter trial validates valerian-
>   lemon balm combination. `verified`
> - **2020s:** Continued widespread use as over-the-counter
>   sleep aid; dietary supplement market estimated at hundreds
>   of millions USD annually. `verified`

**The Diazepam connection:**

> Diazepam (Valium), one of the first widely-prescribed
> benzodiazepines, was named partly to evoke valerian's calming
> reputation. The trade name "Valium" derives from Latin
> *valere* — the same root as *Valeriana*. The benzodiazepines
> were marketed in the 1960s-1970s partly as a more reliable,
> standardized version of valerian's effect. Modern research
> has revealed that valerian and benzodiazepines act on the
> same GABA-A receptor (different binding subunits) — the
> historical naming connection turns out to reflect genuine
> mechanistic kinship. `verified` (etymology); `established`
> (mechanism).

**Modern clinical evidence:**

> Valerian has accumulated solid clinical research:
>
> - **Donath et al. 2000:** Polysomnography study, valerian
>   600mg over 14 days significantly improved slow-wave sleep
>   parameters
> - **Cerny 1999:** Multicenter RCT, valerian + lemon balm
>   combination effective for sleep
> - **Bent et al. 2006 systematic review:** Meta-analysis of
>   16 studies; valerian probably effective for subjective
>   sleep improvement
> - **AAFP Review 2003:** Valerian effective for insomnia at
>   300-600mg standardized extract
> - **Mineo et al. 2017:** Cortical excitability modulation
>   demonstrating CNS effects in humans
> - **Multiple anxiolytic studies in animals:** Valerenic acid
>   and total extract show anti-anxiety effects in elevated
>   plus-maze and similar models
>
> Mechanistically:
> - GABA-A receptor binding (beta subunit, distinct from
>   benzodiazepine gamma-subunit binding)
> - GABA transaminase inhibition (similar to lemon balm)
> - Possible serotonin 5-HT5a partial agonism (Dietz 2005)
> - Adenosine receptor effects
> - Effects on cortical excitability documented
>
> The evidence is moderate — not as robust as ginger for
> nausea or ashwagandha for stress, but comfortably supportive
> of sleep specialty. Heterogeneity in trial designs and
> standardization limits meta-analytic confidence somewhat.
> `established` for sleep effect; `attested` for anxiolytic
> effect.

**Ayurvedic equivalent — *Tagara*:**

> Indian valerian (*V. jatamansi* / *V. wallichii*) is used
> similarly in Ayurveda as *Tagara* or *Sugandhabala*. Used
> for insomnia, anxiety, and *vata* disorders. Same general
> sedative-anxiolytic profile but distinct species. Worth
> noting that the European *V. officinalis* and Indian
> *V. jatamansi* aren't interchangeable in clinical contexts
> but are functionally similar. `attested`

**TCM framing:**

> Various *Valeriana* species used in TCM (e.g., *V. fauriei*,
> *V. jatamansi*) for similar sedative purposes. Less central
> than in Western herbalism but present. `attested`

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **`Sleepy` 5 ceiling established.** Valerian sets the
   catalog ceiling. Future ingredients can join (cannabis/CBD,
   melatonin if added) but won't exceed without rare
   pharmaceutical-strength botanicals. The 1-3 range remains
   for general "calming" herbs; 4-5 reserved for specialty
   sleep agents.

2. **The valerian + lemon balm combination.** Should be
   surfaced strongly in blend-recommendation logic for users
   seeking sleep support. Two complementary GABAergic
   mechanisms; flavor masking; clinical validation.

3. **The paradoxical stimulation note.** Real and worth
   surfacing. About 5-10% of users react opposite to expected;
   user-facing copy should set expectation honestly.

4. **The driving/operating machinery warning.** Unlike most
   catalog ingredients, valerian needs an explicit functional
   warning. UI treatment should make this visible.

5. **The smell expectation.** Funky-cheesy aroma is
   characteristic and surprising. Don't hide it; lean into it
   as part of the ingredient's identity. Users who can't
   tolerate the smell can stick to capsule supplements
   (outside catalog scope) or rely on flavor-masking blends.

6. **The 14-day continuous-use effect.** Donath 2000
   polysomnography study suggests valerian's effect builds
   with continuous nightly use rather than acting acutely.
   This is unusual for sleep herbs and worth surfacing as
   "valerian rewards consistency" framing.

7. **Pairing recommendations:**
   - **Lemon balm** (Cerny 1999 validated combination)
   - **Chamomile** (additive GABAergic + flavor masking)
   - **Lavender** (mild calming additive)
   - **Hops** (*Humulus lupulus* — not in catalog; classic
     European combination)
   - **Passionflower** (additive GABAergic; common modern
     blend)

8. **Schema reconciliation.** Like other root medicinal
   ingredients (turmeric, ashwagandha), valerian needs schema
   that handles longer brewing times and the medicinal-strength
   considerations.

9. **Indian valerian distinction.** *V. jatamansi* /
   *V. wallichii* is a distinct species used in Ayurveda.
   Catalog ingredient is European *V. officinalis*; Ayurvedic
   *Tagara* is technically different though functionally similar.
   Could become a future split per Principle #18 if Ayurvedic
   audience grows; current single entry is appropriate.

10. **The benzodiazepine adjacency.** Valerian's mechanism is
    similar enough to benzodiazepines that the historical
    naming connection (Valium ← *valere* ← Valeriana) reflects
    real chemistry. Worth surfacing in user-facing copy as
    "natural cousin to prescription sleep medications" without
    overclaiming equivalence.

## Addendum — prose claim audit (2026-08-23)

**Onset takes weeks, not one night — sourced.** The facts list compares
valerian's onset to an SSRI rather than a sedative. That matches the
trial literature: the studies reporting the clearest effects used
nightly dosing at 300-600 mg for two to four weeks, and single-dose
evidence is inconsistent. The mechanism offered is gradual modulation
of GABA receptor sensitivity rather than acute sedation.

This matters for the cup more than most facts do — a reader who tries
one evening's infusion and concludes it does nothing has run the
experiment the evidence says will fail. `attested`
