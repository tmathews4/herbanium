## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The partial-oxidation tea — the most diverse category in tea,
> spanning green-leaning florals to black-leaning roasted cups.

**Short description:**

> Oolong is a semi-oxidized true tea — leaves are partially
> oxidized (anywhere from 12% to 80%), arrested with heat (*shaqing*),
> then rolled, often roasted, and dried. This wide oxidation range
> means "oolong" spans floral green-adjacent teas (Tie Guan Yin
> Qing Xiang, Taiwan high-mountain) to deep roasted black-adjacent
> teas (Wuyi rock oolongs, Taiwanese charcoal-roasted Dongding).
> No single sensory description covers the category. Produced
> primarily in Fujian, China; Guangdong; and Taiwan — each region
> with distinctive styles and cultural traditions.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes (canonical medium oxidation):**

- floral
- fruity
- roasted
- sweet
- complex

**Regional variation:**

> - **Anxi Tie Guan Yin (green style, ~15-25% oxidation):**
>   orchid, floral, buttery, gardenia
> - **Traditional Tie Guan Yin / Taiwanese Dongding (40-50%
>   oxidation, roasted):** baked pastry, dried fruit, nuts, honey
> - **Wuyi rock oolong / Da Hong Pao (50-70% oxidation, heavy
>   roast):** roasted stone fruit, dark caramel, wet granite,
>   "rock rhyme" (*yan yun*) mineral backbone
> - **Dan Cong (Phoenix oolong, varies):** highly aromatic —
>   honey orchid, almond, duck shit (yes, really — the name is
>   more colorful than the tea), mi lan (honey orchid), yu lan
>   (magnolia)
> - **Taiwan high-mountain (light oxidation, unroasted):** creamy,
>   floral, sweet stone fruits, sometimes compared to peach and
>   butter
> - **Oriental Beauty / Dongfang Meiren (70%+ oxidation):** honey,
>   ripe peach, muscatel — insect-induced terpene flavor similar
>   to Darjeeling second flush (same biochemistry!)

**Mouthfeel:**

> Varies with oxidation and roast. Lightly oxidized oolongs are
> bright, clean, creamy. Heavily roasted oolongs are full-bodied,
> layered, with lingering mineral or caramel aftertaste. Most
> quality oolongs share a characteristic *hui gan* returning
> sweetness — similar to pu-erh but different quality (floral-
> sweet rather than earthy-sweet).

**Basic tastes:**

> - `bitter` (1-2) — generally lower than green tea due to partial
>   oxidation reducing catechin astringency
> - `astringent` (1-2) — modest; quality oolong integrates
>   astringency into structure rather than announcing it
> - `sweet` (2) — characteristic *hui gan* returning sweetness

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|

> **Oolong is the "focus tea" of true teas.** Sencha, gyokuro,
> and dragonwell also score high on focus, but oolong's
> partial-oxidation produces a distinctive "centered alertness"
> that many tea drinkers prefer to green tea's sharper focus or
> black tea's more stimulating energy. Meditation traditions in
> Fujian and Taiwan specifically favor oolong for this reason.

| sources | traditional, Wuyi tea master practice |

### 6d. Time-axis behavior & gongfu note

> Oolong is the quintessential gongfu tea alongside pu-erh.
> Traditional gongfu practice: 5-7g per 100ml gaiwan, 15-20s
> first infusion, +5-10s per subsequent infusion, 5-10+
> infusions from one dose. Each infusion reveals a different
> aspect:
>
> - Infusions 1-2: opening aromatics, light body
> - Infusions 3-5: peak character, full expression
> - Infusions 6-8: depth, minerals, sweetness
> - Infusions 9+: fading into calm brothy finish
>
> Western-style brewing (single 3-4 min steep) loses this
> evolution. For oolong drinkers, Western brewing is the
> "starter method" — most serious oolong drinkers graduate to
> gongfu within a year or two of exploring the category.
>
> Ball-rolled oolongs (Tie Guan Yin, Taiwan high-mountain)
> unfurl gradually — expect 3-4 short infusions before the leaves
> are fully open and releasing full flavor. Strip-rolled oolongs
> (Dan Cong, Wuyi rock) open faster but have shorter peak
> windows.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**Known interactions:**

> Standard tea cautions — caffeine (~37mg/cup), iron absorption
> interference at meals, fluoride at very heavy consumption.
> No oolong-specific concerns. `established`

**Dosage cautions:**

> 3-5 cups per day is within well-studied range. Gongfu brewing
> of very high-leaf-ratio oolong can deliver higher total caffeine
> per session if all infusions are consumed — worth noting for
> evening drinkers.

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"Oolong causes weight loss"** — Modest evidence at best;
>   the "oolong weight loss" marketing overstates. Like other
>   teas, oolong may modestly support metabolism at high intake
>   but isn't a weight-loss intervention. `folk`
> - **"Oriental Beauty's bug-bitten origin is a defect"** — It's
>   the point. The jassid-induced terpene chemistry that produces
>   Oriental Beauty's honey-peach flavor is the same mechanism as
>   Darjeeling muscatel. Organic growing practices for this tea
>   specifically tolerate insect presence. `established`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Origin — contested:**

> The origin of oolong tea is debated between Fujian tea regions.
> The two main candidates:
>
> - **Wuyishan origin (Northern Fujian):** The Ming-dynasty Beiyuan
>   tea garden produced "Long Tuan Feng Cake" (Dragon-Phoenix Tribute
>   Cake) tea until Ming Emperor Zhu Yuanzhang banned cake-tea
>   production. The region then developed loose-leaf production
>   with partial oxidation — potentially the first oolong.
>   `attested`
> - **Anxi origin (Southern Fujian):** The Iron Goddess Tie Guan
>   Yin tradition, with its legendary origin story of a farmer
>   finding a unique tea plant near an iron statue of Guanyin
>   bodhisattva, dates to the 18th century. `attested`
>
> "Wu Long" (乌龙) literally means "black dragon," referring either
> to the dark, curled leaf shape or to Beiyuan tea heritage.
> The two regions still argue; modern scholars generally place the
> emergence of oolong-style tea in 17th-18th century Fujian.
> `attested`

**The four classic oolong regions:**

> 1. **Wuyishan (武夷山), Northern Fujian** — Yancha (rock tea).
>    Famous teas: Da Hong Pao (Big Red Robe), Rou Gui (Cinnamon),
>    Shui Xian (Water Sprite), Tie Luo Han (Iron Arhat). Grown on
>    mineral-rich rocky cliffs; heavy oxidation (50-70%) + heavy
>    charcoal roast. The "rock rhyme" (*yan yun*) mineral aftertaste
>    defines this style.
> 2. **Anxi (安溪), Southern Fujian** — Tie Guan Yin country.
>    Famous teas: Tie Guan Yin (Iron Goddess), Ben Shan (Source
>    Mountain), Mao Xie (Hairy Crab). Softer, more floral,
>    ball-rolled. Modern Anxi has shifted toward the very lightly
>    oxidized Qing Xiang (清香) style developed in the 1990s.
> 3. **Phoenix Mountains (凤凰山), Guangdong** — Dan Cong
>    (Phoenix Single Bush) oolongs. Made from single ancient
>    trees, each producing a distinct aromatic profile. Named by
>    aromatic type: Mi Lan Xiang (honey orchid), Ya Shi Xiang
>    (duck shit, named for a field accident), Gui Hua Xiang
>    (osmanthus), etc. Strip-rolled, medium oxidation.
> 4. **Taiwan** — Adopted oolong traditions from Fujian in 1855
>    when Lin Fengchi brought Qingxin oolong plants from Wuyi to
>    Dongding Village, Nantou County. Taiwan now produces a huge
>    range: Dongding, Ali Shan, Li Shan, Shan Lin Xi (high-mountain
>    oolongs), Bao Zhong (light/unoxidized), Oriental Beauty
>    (heavily oxidized, bug-bitten).
> `verified`

**Taiwan's "Formosa Oolong" story:**

> Scottish merchant John Dodd established the first Taiwanese
> tea export company in Wanhua, Taipei, in 1867, marketing
> Taiwanese oolong to Western markets as "Formosa Oolong."
> (Formosa was the Portuguese colonial name for Taiwan.) Oolong
> became Taiwan's top export, outranking sugar and camphor. The
> 1867 start of Taiwan's commercial tea industry mirrors James
> Taylor's 1867 start of Ceylon's — both enabled by the Treaty of
> Tientsin (1860) opening Chinese/Asian ports to trade. `verified`

**The chemistry — what partial oxidation does:**

> In oolong processing, leaves are partly oxidized — typically 8%
> to 80% — before heat (*shaqing*) halts the enzyme reactions.
> The exact point at which oxidation is arrested determines the
> color, aroma, and flavor. During this partial oxidation:
>
> - Catechins progressively convert to theaflavins, then partially
>   to thearubigins (stopped before full conversion unlike black
>   tea)
> - Floral terpenoids — especially linalool, geraniol, nerolidol
>   — increase dramatically (this is the chemistry behind the
>   characteristic oolong aroma; documented in 2024 Fujian
>   Agriculture and Forestry research)
> - Green leaf volatiles (*z*-3-hexenol and related) reduce
> - L-theanine is partially preserved (less than green tea, more
>   than black — average 6.09 mg/g per Csupor 2016)
> - Caffeine content unaffected by oxidation but varies by cultivar
>
> Roasting (for roasted oolongs) adds another layer of chemistry:
> Maillard reactions produce caramel-roasted notes, and slow
> charcoal roasting develops the mineral-complex character that
> Wuyi rock oolongs are prized for. `verified`

**Gongfu cha — the oolong ritual:**

> Gongfu cha (功夫茶, "skill-tea" or "attention-tea") is the
> multi-infusion tea-brewing tradition that developed around
> oolong in Fujian and Taiwan. Small clay or porcelain pots,
> high leaf-to-water ratios, short steep times, many infusions.
> The practice spread through Chinese tea culture and is now the
> default for serious oolong and pu-erh drinkers worldwide.
> Gongfu brewing isn't a ceremony (in the Japanese sense) — it's
> a practical brewing method developed to extract maximum
> character from high-quality oolong leaves. `verified`

**TCM framing:**

> Oolongs span a wide range of TCM warming character:
> - Lightly oxidized/unroasted oolongs: neutral to mildly cooling
> - Medium oxidation: neutral
> - Heavily oxidized and roasted: warming, though less than black
> Most tea traditions classify oolong as "balanced" — neither
> strongly warming nor cooling, suitable year-round. `attested`

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **Oolong is really a category, not an ingredient.** Tie Guan
   Yin, Da Hong Pao, Dan Cong Mi Lan Xiang, Oriental Beauty, and
   Dongding are sensorially and chemically different teas that
   happen to share "semi-oxidized" in common. Herbanium's current
   single `oolong` entry is honest but under-resolved. Future
   catalog expansion should consider splitting by major style —
   at minimum: `tie-guan-yin`, `wuyi-rock` (Da Hong Pao etc.), and
   `dan-cong`.

2. **Oxidation level matters more than the word "oolong."** The
   effect profile, brewing parameters, and flavor vocabulary for a
   15% oxidation Qing Xiang Tie Guan Yin have more in common with
   sencha than with a 70% oxidation Da Hong Pao. Using one set of
   data for all oolong flattens this.

3. **Roast level is independent of oxidation.** A medium-oxidation
   oolong can be unroasted (modern Anxi Tie Guan Yin) or heavily
   roasted (traditional Tie Guan Yin / Taiwanese Dongding). The
   roast drives much of the color and a big chunk of the flavor.
   Currently not represented in the data model.

4. **Gongfu brewing is the default for quality oolong.** Western
   brewing parameters (single long steep) work but lose much of
   what makes oolong distinctive. Herbanium's brewing-guidance
   copy should probably surface this — "this tea rewards multiple
   short infusions" kind of hint.

5. **Caffeine averaging is misleading.** Taiwan high-mountain
   oolongs often under 25mg/cup; Wuyi rock oolongs can exceed
   50mg. Using 37mg as a middle estimate is reasonable but lossy.

6. **Oriental Beauty's jassid chemistry shares biochemistry with
   Darjeeling muscatel.** Worth noting in cross-references — the
   same insect-mediated flavor development operates in both teas.
   Tea education benefits from this connection.
