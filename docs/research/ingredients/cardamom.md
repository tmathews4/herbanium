## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The Queen of Spices — aromatic, camphor-bright, simultaneously
> warming and cooling, and central to three of the world's most
> enduring tea and coffee traditions.

**Short description:**

> Cardamom is the dried seed pod of *Elettaria cardamomum*, a
> herbaceous perennial in the ginger family (Zingiberaceae)
> native to the Western Ghats of southern India and Sri Lanka.
> Used for at least 5,000 years in Ayurvedic medicine and across
> human spice trade since Sumerian times. Today it commands the
> third-highest price per weight among common spices (after
> saffron and vanilla), with Guatemala producing 70% of global
> supply since the 1980s. Uniquely warming-and-cooling —
> classical Ayurveda classifies it as having *shita virya*
> (cooling potency) while modern TCM frames it as moderately
> warming. Central to three iconic beverages: Indian masala
> chai, Arabic coffee (*gahwa*), and its lesser-known but very
> old role in Scandinavian baking (brought by Vikings via trade
> with the Byzantine Empire).

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- aromatic
- camphor
- citrus
- sweet
- complex

**Aroma notes:**

> One of the most distinctive aromas in the spice cabinet. The
> dominant compounds are 1,8-cineole (eucalyptol, also present
> in eucalyptus leaves — accounts for the menthol-cool quality)
> and α-terpinyl acetate (sweet-floral-woody). Myrcene and
> limonene contribute citrus brightness. The result is an aroma
> that reads simultaneously as: eucalyptus-fresh, lemon-bright,
> floral-sweet, and spice-warm — a full spectrum from a single
> compact pod.
>
> Fresh pods smell markedly different from old/stale cardamom.
> Quality cardamom should smell vividly aromatic from several
> feet away when a pod is crushed; faded cardamom produces a
> dusty, one-note impression. Freshness matters more for
> cardamom than for most spices.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Initial cool sensation (eucalyptol on the palate) gives way to
> warming spice character as essential oils disperse. Very
> aromatic mouthfeel — cardamom announces itself long after the
> sip is over. Slight astringency from the woody pod if pod is
> included rather than just seeds. Sometimes described as
> "perfumed" when used in excess.

**Basic tastes:**

> - `aromatic` (5) — most aromatic of the warming spices;
>   dominates the sensory profile
> - `sweet` (2) — modest natural sweetness; more in the aromatic
>   register than the literal sugar sense
> - `pungent` (2) — present but softer than ginger or cloves
> - `bitter` (1) — slight; mostly in crushed whole pods
> - `cool` (2) — genuine cooling sensation from eucalyptol

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [95, 100] | traditional | Full boil; essential oils extract aggressively at high temperature |
| **time range (seconds)** | [300, 600] | traditional | 5-10 min for crushed pods; 3-5 min for ground. Extraction accelerates dramatically when pods are cracked/crushed before steeping |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 0 | universal | Caffeine-free |
| **dose** | 3-5 pods (lightly crushed) per 250ml | traditional | Small amount of pods packs significant flavor; ground form: ~1/4 tsp per cup |

> **Crush the pods:** Whole cardamom pods are nearly inert in
> tea — the seeds inside contain the aromatic oils, and intact
> pods resist water penetration. Light crushing (pressing with
> flat of knife, or pestle) to crack the pod without pulverizing
> is the canonical preparation. This releases seeds while
> keeping them from floating as dust. `verified`

> **Vocabulary stress test:** Cardamom tests the paired-effect
> vocabulary — the `warming`/`cooling` pair is explicitly designed
> as opposites, and cardamom genuinely earns both (at 3 and 2
> respectively). The vocabulary accommodates this: an ingredient
> can have both paired effects at non-zero strengths when the
> underlying mechanism is physiologically complex. Cardamom's
> warming comes from spice-family character; cooling comes from
> eucalyptol. These are real, co-present, and documented across
> traditions (TCM warming, Ayurvedic cooling). Vocabulary holds.

### 6d. The three canonical preparations

> Cardamom appears in three distinct iconic beverage traditions,
> each with distinctive preparation:
>
> - **Masala chai (Indian/South Asian):** Green cardamom pods +
>   cinnamon + ginger + cloves + black pepper + black tea + milk
>   + sugar, all simmered together. Cardamom provides the
>   aromatic anchor. 2-3 pods per cup typical.
> - **Arabic/Bedouin gahwa (*قهوة*):** Lightly-roasted coffee
>   beans ground coarsely with significant cardamom (often 1:1
>   ratio by weight in traditional preparations), brewed in
>   *dallah* pots, served without sugar but sometimes with dates.
>   The cardamom is the dominant flavor; coffee plays supporting
>   role. Cultural centerpiece across Arabian Peninsula.
> - **Scandinavian cardamom buns (*kardemummabullar*, *pulla*):**
>   Ground green cardamom baked into yeasted buns — part of an
>   800+ year Nordic spice tradition dating to Viking trade
>   routes through Byzantium.
>
> These three traditions represent the primary global cardamom
> consumption vectors. Herbanium blend recommendations should
> recognize the chai cluster (cardamom + cinnamon + ginger +
> cloves) as particularly well-established.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**Known interactions:**

> - **Anticoagulants:** Cardamom may have mild antiplatelet
>   effects; theoretical additive bleeding risk at high doses.
>   Clinically significant only at supplement doses, not tea.
>   `attested`
> - **Blood pressure medications:** Limited evidence suggests
>   cardamom can modestly lower blood pressure (Verma 2009);
>   theoretical additive hypotension at supplement doses. `attested`
> - **Gallstones:** Ayurvedic tradition recommends avoiding
>   cardamom with gallstones. Mechanism: stimulates bile flow.
>   `folk`

**Pregnancy:**

> Ayurveda specifically advises caution with cardamom in
> pregnancy, especially in large medicinal quantities. Culinary
> use (in chai, baked goods) is universal across South Asian
> pregnancy and considered safe. Modern evidence doesn't support
> any pregnancy concerns at normal culinary/tea doses. `attested`

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"Cardamom is addictive"** — Folklore claim referring to
>   strong craving potential; no pharmacological addiction
>   mechanism. `folk`
> - **"Cardamom cures cancer"** — Some pre-clinical anti-tumor
>   signals; no clinical translation. Standard overreach pattern.
>   `folk`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origin:**

> *Elettaria cardamomum* is native to the mid-to-high elevation
> wet forests of the Western Ghats of Kerala and Karnataka states
> in southern India, extending into Sri Lanka. The "Cardamom
> Hills" (*Elaimalai*) region of Kerala was named for wild
> cardamom abundance. Grows naturally 2500-5000 ft elevation in
> humid tropical forests with 1200-6000mm annual rainfall.
> Traditionally cultivated in forest-garden systems that preserve
> the tree canopy (cardamom prefers 50-70% shade). `verified`

**Historical timeline — 5,000+ years:**

> - **~3000 BCE:** Earliest textual reference found in Sumerian
>   records (not definitively identified species but widely
>   cited). `attested`
> - **~4th century BCE:** Kautilya's *Arthashastra* mentions
>   cardamom (*truti*, *ela*) as offering in ceremonies. Charaka
>   Samhita documents medicinal use. `verified`
> - **Ancient Egypt:** Cardamom chewed for oral hygiene and
>   breath freshening; used in perfumery and embalming.
>   `attested`
> - **Greek and Roman antiquity:** Hippocrates wrote about
>   cardamom's digestive benefits. Romans called it *cardamomum*,
>   derived from Greek *kardámōmon* (κάρδαμωμον, a compound of
>   "cress" + "amomum"). Imported from India via Arabian and
>   Egyptian middlemen. `verified`
> - **Early medieval period (~800-1000 CE):** Cardamom reaches
>   Scandinavia via Viking trade routes through Byzantium —
>   explaining the otherwise surprising centrality of cardamom
>   in Nordic baking traditions. Norwegian/Swedish
>   *kardemummabullar* trace to this era. `verified`
> - **Medieval Arabia:** Central to *gahwa* (Arabic coffee)
>   tradition; Bedouin hospitality tradition of offering
>   cardamom-rich coffee becomes cultural cornerstone across
>   Arabian Peninsula. `verified`
> - **Colonial period (1500s-1800s):** Portuguese, Dutch, and
>   British East India Companies fight for control of cardamom
>   trade; Sri Lanka and southern India remain the sole sources
>   until industrial-era plantation development. `verified`
> - **Pre-WWI:** A German plantation owner introduces cardamom
>   to Guatemala, seeking to undercut Indian production.
>   Cardamom thrives in Guatemala's highland cloud forests —
>   same elevation and humidity as Kerala. `verified`
> - **1979-1980:** Guatemala surpasses India in world
>   cardamom production. `verified`
> - **21st century:** Guatemala produces 70% of global supply;
>   India 15-20%; Sri Lanka, Tanzania, Vietnam, Indonesia
>   balance. Climate change increasingly threatens production
>   as temperature/rainfall patterns shift. `verified`

**The Guatemala story:**

> One of the most remarkable agricultural transfers of the
> 20th century. Cardamom arrived in Guatemala pre-WWI and
> thrived — by 1979 Guatemala had overtaken India, cardamom's
> original homeland, in world production. Cardamom is now
> called *oro verde* ("green gold") in Guatemala; it's among
> the country's top agricultural exports despite being largely
> invisible to Guatemalan consumers (production is almost
> entirely for export). Most cardamom in American grocery
> stores, Middle Eastern coffee, and Scandinavian bakeries
> originates in Guatemalan highlands, not India. `verified`

**TCM framing:**

> Cardamom (*Bái Dòu Kòu* for the white-bleached variant, or
> green cardamom in general terms) is used in TCM for:
>
> - Moving qi and relieving digestive stagnation
> - Warming the middle (digestive center) — especially with
>   cold-pattern conditions
> - Occasional use for dampness reduction
>
> Not as central in TCM as in Ayurveda; TCM uses are generally
> digestive-focused. `verified`

**Ayurvedic framing:**

> *Ela* is one of the most important spices in Ayurveda.
> Classical classification:
>
> - **Rasa (taste):** *Madhura* (sweet) + *Katu* (pungent)
> - **Virya (potency):** *Shita* (cooling)
> - **Vipaka (post-digestive effect):** *Katu* (pungent)
> - **Dosha effect:** Pacifies *Kapha* and *Vata*; can
>   aggravate *Pitta* in excess but generally well-tolerated
>
> Used for digestion (*agni*-kindling without overheating),
> nausea, bad breath, urinary complaints, heart support,
> hiccups. Component in countless formulations and in daily
> Ayurvedic kitchen practice. The dual warming-and-cooling
> character (*katu* taste + *shita* potency) makes cardamom
> uniquely versatile across constitutions. `verified`

**Cultural roles:**

> - **South Asian cuisine:** Masala chai anchor; biryanis and
>   pulaos (often whole pods added to the rice); Indian sweets
>   (*gulab jamun*, *kheer*, *kulfi*); garam masala component.
> - **Arab cuisine:** Gahwa coffee anchor; Yemeni *hawaij* spice
>   blend; Turkish coffee sometimes; various stews.
> - **Persian cuisine:** Stews, rice dishes, tea preparations.
> - **Scandinavian:** Cardamom buns, cakes, breads; more
>   cardamom per capita consumed in Sweden than in India.
> - **Global modern:** Increasingly in cocktails (gin,
>   aquavit), boutique tea blends, high-end baking.
> `verified`

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **Green vs. black cardamom as separate ingredients?** Most
   commercial "cardamom" is green (*Elettaria cardamomum*).
   Black cardamom (*Amomum subulatum*) is a distinct plant with
   a dramatically different profile — smoky, camphor-heavy,
   savory. They are not substitutes. Current `cardamom` entry
   should be interpreted as green; worth documenting explicitly.
   Future catalog expansion could add `cardamom-black` for
   savory/biryani contexts.

2. **Vocabulary paired-effects test passed.** Cardamom earning
   both `warming` 3 and `cooling` 2 demonstrates that the paired-
   effect vocabulary can accommodate physiologically complex
   ingredients. This is a productive finding — the vocabulary
   doesn't force choosing between warming and cooling when an
   ingredient genuinely has both qualities.

3. **Freshness matters more than for most ingredients.** Cardamom
   aromatic compounds (especially 1,8-cineole) are volatile and
   degrade within 6-12 months of grinding. Whole pods keep for
   2+ years. User-facing copy should prefer "whole pods, crushed
   at time of use" for best experience. Not currently in the
   data model.

4. **Three distinct cultural preparation contexts.** The chai
   cluster (Indian), gahwa cluster (Arab), and kardemummabullar
   cluster (Scandinavian) are different enough that a single
   "pairing recommendation" engine should ideally distinguish
   them. For now, flagging for future blend-recommendation work.

5. **The Guatemala/India production story.** An unusual case
   where the largest producer (Guatemala) is not the country of
   origin (India). User-facing sourcing information, if ever
   surfaced, should note both — the *traditional* source is
   India, the *most-likely actual source* of what they're
   drinking is Guatemala.

6. **Flavor vocabulary: `aromatic` as a category.** Cardamom is
   the most aromatic ingredient in the catalog so far — even
   more than rose, which is floral-aromatic but more delicate.
   Worth considering whether `aromatic` deserves its own top-
   level flavor category when the catalog has more of these
   entries (vanilla also fits; cinnamon to a lesser degree).

## Addendum — `energy` kept, sourced as tradition (2026-08-02)

An audit flagged `energy` as unsourced. Unlike rose or lemongrass —
where the evidence actively contradicted the claim — cardamom's rests
on documented traditional use rather than on a trial, and that is worth
recording precisely rather than deleting.

> Cardamom seeds have long been used as a stimulant and tonic, valued
> in Ayurvedic practice for lifting the spirits and restoring vitality.
> Reviewed in *Health-Promoting Effects of Cardamom (Elettaria
> cardamomum)* and *The effect of Elettaria cardamomum on the metabolic
> syndrome: narrative review* —
> https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8917848/

**No controlled human trial tests cardamom for alertness, fatigue or
energy.** The modern evidence base is metabolic and cardiovascular. So
this is a tradition-sourced claim, on the same footing as the other
`sources: traditional` entries in this catalogue — not a clinical one,
and it shouldn't be presented as though it were.
