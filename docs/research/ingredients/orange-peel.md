## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> Warming citrus peel — the workhorse aromatic of mulled wines, chai bases, and cozy spice blends.

**Short description:**

> Orange peel is the dried rind of *Citrus sinensis* (sweet) or
> *C. aurantium* (bitter), used as a flavoring across tea
> traditions. In Chinese herbalism, aged tangerine peel
> (*chen pi*) is one of the most valued digestive herbs —
> peels are stored for years, and the older the peel, the
> higher the price. In the West, orange peel anchors mulled
> wine, gluhwein, masala chai bases, and most "cozy" or
> "Christmas" blend formulas. The peel's volatile oil is
> dominated by limonene (~95%), with smaller amounts of
> myrcene, linalool, and citral — giving it a brighter,
> sweeter register than bergamot, and a warming character
> when paired with spices. The white pith carries bitter
> flavonoids (hesperidin, naringenin) that contribute the
> "bittersweet" backbone; cheap orange peel is mostly pith
> and reads harshly bitter. Traditional digestive use is
> well-attested across both Chinese and European
> pharmacopoeias; modern studies confirm mild prokinetic
> effects (it nudges stomach emptying and gut motility) at
> tea-relevant doses. The peel itself contains no caffeine.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- citrus
- aromatic
- sweet
- bittersweet

**Aroma notes:**

> Warmer and sweeter than bergamot or lemon — a rounder,
> orange-grove top note over a slightly bitter pith
> backbone. Reads as "Christmas market" when paired with
> cinnamon and clove; as "bright digestive" when paired
> with ginger or fennel.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Light to medium. The pith adds a slight astringent grip
> that gives the cup more backbone than bergamot alone
> would. In a chai base, it bridges between the spice oils
> and the milk fat.

### 6d. Time-axis behavior

> Orange peel has a clear inversion past ~6 minutes:
> volatile oils peak and start to evaporate while pith
> bitter flavonoids continue to extract. Sweetness drops
> out faster than bitterness does — so a long steep
> doesn't give "more orange," it gives "less orange,
> more pith."

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**headsUp field:**

> Orange peel may interact with medications metabolized
> by CYP3A4 (similar mechanism to grapefruit, weaker
> effect). Bitter orange (*aurantium*) contains synephrine
> and shouldn't be combined with stimulants or used at
> high doses by people with cardiovascular conditions.
> Sweet orange peel at tea levels is safe.

**Dosage cautions:**

> A teaspoon per cup is generous; more becomes harshly
> pithy and bitter. Aged *chen pi* is dosed lower — a
> pinch is plenty.

**Not a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> "Bitter orange burns fat" — synephrine has weak
> stimulant effects, but the supplement-industry weight-loss
> claims are far overstated for tea-level doses.

## Addendum — `warming` removed (2026-08-03)

An audit found `warming` 2–3 shipped at every brew point — the second
largest value in the profile — with no §6 row prescribing it. §5
prescribes `comfort` 1 instead, and its gloss is precise about why:

> | comfort | 1 | The **"warming"** register that makes it the
> chai/mulled-wine staple |

The quotation marks are doing real work. The doc is describing the
register orange peel *belongs to* in a blend, not claiming the peel
generates heat. Since `warming` and `comfort` were separated, that
distinction has a home: `warming` is thermogenic — gingerol and
piperine acting on TRPV1, measurable body heat — and citrus peel has
no such constituent. Its aromatics are limonene and linalool, which
carry the `uplifting` claim §5 already rates at 2.

What orange peel actually contributes to a mulled wine or a chai is
the *association* — sweet, spiced, seasonal. That is `comfort`, at the
modest 1 the research gives it, and the profile already carries it.

Removed. `uplifting` 2 and `digestive` 2 are the sourced picture,
with `comfort` 1 alongside.
