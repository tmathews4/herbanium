## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The bark spice — sweet, warming, ubiquitous, and quietly
> contentious because the two species sold as "cinnamon" have
> different chemistry and different safety profiles.

**Short description:**

> Cinnamon is the dried inner bark of trees in the *Cinnamomum*
> genus. Two species are commercially significant: *Cinnamomum
> verum* (Ceylon cinnamon, "true cinnamon") and *Cinnamomum
> cassia* (Chinese cassia, "Saigon cinnamon," "Indonesian
> cassia"). Ceylon has a delicate, complex, sweetly nuanced
> flavor; cassia is more intense, peppery-warm, and the more
> common commercial form in US/EU markets. The two species
> differ significantly in coumarin content — a compound that can
> stress the liver at high doses. Cassia contains ~1% coumarin;
> Ceylon contains ~0.004% (250x less). This matters for daily/
> therapeutic use but rarely for occasional culinary use.
> Documented as a tea/chai/medicine ingredient for at least
> 4,000 years, traded along the most ancient maritime spice
> routes.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- sweet
- warm
- woody
- aromatic
- spicy

**Ceylon vs. Cassia sensory difference:**

> - **Ceylon cinnamon:** Lighter color (tan-brown), thinner bark
>   that crumbles easily, complex sweetness with floral and
>   citrus undertones, gentle warmth. ~50-65% cinnamaldehyde.
>   Often described as "refined" or "tea-grade" cinnamon.
> - **Cassia cinnamon:** Darker red-brown color, thicker hard
>   bark, more intense single-note flavor, distinct heat with
>   peppery edge. ~85-90% cinnamaldehyde. The "cinnamon flavor"
>   most Americans recognize.
>
> Side-by-side, the difference is dramatic — many cinnamon
> enthusiasts who grew up with cassia find Ceylon almost
> unrecognizable on first taste. (Cinnamomum verum extract trial
> 2025; Healthline)

**Aroma notes:**

> Sweetly aromatic, deeply warming. The dominant compound across
> both species is cinnamaldehyde (the eponymous chemical),
> supplemented by eugenol (clove-like), linalool (floral),
> cinnamyl acetate (lighter sweet-spicy), and various
> sesquiterpenes. Ceylon's lower cinnamaldehyde concentration
> allows other aromatic compounds to express, producing
> complexity; cassia's high cinnamaldehyde dominates and creates
> the assertive single-character intensity.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Subtle warming heat that develops slowly across the cup.
> Slight astringency and drying quality from tannins. Cinnamon
> oil's slight numbing effect on the tongue is sometimes
> noticeable in concentrated preparations. Less pungent than
> ginger; less drying than turmeric.

**Basic tastes:**

> - `sweet` (3) — characteristic; one of the few warming spices
>   that genuinely tastes sweet
> - `bitter` (1) — slight; mostly in cassia
> - `astringent` (2) — modest tannin content
> - `pungent` (2) — mild peppery quality, especially in cassia

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [95, 100] | traditional | Full boil; cinnamaldehyde and other actives extract well at high temperature |
| **time range (seconds)** | [300, 600] | traditional | 5-10 min for stick cinnamon; 3-5 min for ground. Stick cinnamon needs longer because compound extraction from intact bark is slower |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 0 | universal | Caffeine-free |
| **dose** | 1 stick or 1 tsp ground per 250ml | traditional | Approximately 2-3g cinnamon per cup |

> **Stick vs. ground:** Stick cinnamon (cinnamon quills) extracts
> more slowly but produces a cleaner flavor — common for chai and
> Mexican coffee preparations. Ground cinnamon extracts faster
> but the powder doesn't fully dissolve, leaving sediment.
> Quality cassia stick from Saigon and Indonesia is widely
> available; quality Ceylon stick is more expensive and more
> often broken/curled rather than tightly rolled.

>

> - **Vietnamese coffee:** Saigon cinnamon variant featured.
> - **Apple-cinnamon:** A culturally pervasive pairing across
>   Western, Eastern European, and increasingly global contexts.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**The Ceylon vs. Cassia coumarin issue:**

> This is the most important practical safety information about
> cinnamon:
>
> - **Cassia (most commercial cinnamon):** Contains ~1% coumarin
>   by weight. A teaspoon (~2.5g) provides ~25mg coumarin.
> - **Ceylon ("true cinnamon"):** Contains ~0.004% coumarin —
>   approximately 250x less. The same teaspoon provides
>   ~0.1mg coumarin.
> - **EFSA tolerable daily intake (TDI) for coumarin:** 0.1 mg
>   per kg body weight per day. For a 70kg adult, ~7mg/day.
> - **Implication:** A single teaspoon of cassia exceeds the
>   EFSA TDI for an average adult. Ceylon, even at therapeutic
>   doses (3-6g/day), stays well under the limit.
>
> Coumarin at very high doses can cause hepatotoxicity. The risk
> is real but applies primarily to:
>
> 1. Daily long-term use of cassia (months/years of >1 tsp daily)
> 2. Therapeutic supplementation (cinnamon capsules at 1g+ daily)
> 3. People with pre-existing liver issues
> 4. Combinations with other liver-stressing substances
>
> For occasional culinary use (a sprinkle on coffee, in a
> chai, in baked goods) cassia is fine for most healthy adults.
> For daily cinnamon tea drinking or supplementation, **Ceylon
> is meaningfully safer.** (Multiple sources; EFSA guideline)

**Known interactions:**

> - **Anticoagulants (warfarin etc.):** Coumarin in cassia is
>   structurally related to warfarin; theoretical additive
>   bleeding risk at high cassia doses. Ceylon's low coumarin
>   minimizes this concern. `attested`
> - **Diabetes medications:** Cinnamon may modestly lower blood
>   glucose; theoretical additive hypoglycemia with insulin or
>   sulfonylureas at supplement doses. `attested`
> - **Statins (cholesterol drugs):** Some reports of interaction;
>   not well-characterized. `folk`

**Pregnancy:**

> Culinary cinnamon (in cooking, sprinkled in foods) is safe in
> pregnancy across all traditions. Therapeutic doses (≥1g/day
> daily as supplement) are not recommended in pregnancy due to
> potential uterine-stimulating effects (the ancient connection
> between "cinnamon as abortifacient" exists in some traditional
> texts but is generally folk; modern caution is conservative
> rather than evidence-based for normal doses). For tea use, Ceylon
> at ~1 cup/day is widely considered safe in pregnancy. `attested`

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"Cinnamon causes mouth ulcers"** — Some individuals are
>   sensitive to cinnamaldehyde and develop contact stomatitis;
>   a small but real subgroup. Not a general population
>   concern. `attested`
> - **"Cinnamon prevents Alzheimer's"** — Pre-clinical research
>   shows some neuroprotective effects of cinnamaldehyde; no
>   clinical confirmation. `folk`
> - **"Cinnamon cures diabetes"** — Modest blood sugar effects
>   are real but not curative; cinnamon should not replace
>   diabetes medications. `folk`
> - **"All cinnamon is the same"** — Genuinely false; the Ceylon
>   vs. cassia distinction matters chemically and for safety.
>   `verified` (counter-claim)

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origins:**

> - **Ceylon cinnamon (*Cinnamomum verum*):** Native to Sri
>   Lanka (formerly Ceylon) and southern India. Sri Lanka still
>   produces the majority of true Ceylon cinnamon globally.
>   Ceylon Cinnamon is GI-protected by Sri Lanka.
> - **Cassia cinnamon (*Cinnamomum cassia*):** Native to
>   southern China and Vietnam; cultivated across South China,
>   Vietnam, Indonesia. Vietnamese "Saigon cinnamon" and
>   Indonesian "Korintje cinnamon" are commercially distinct
>   variants of cassia.
>
> The trees are botanical cousins but distinctly different
> species, native to different regions, with notably different
> chemistry. `verified`

**Historical timeline — among the oldest spice trade goods:**

> - **~2000 BCE:** Cinnamon mentioned in Egyptian texts as an
>   embalming spice; archaeological cinnamon residue found in
>   Phoenician trade flask in northern Israel (Stager 2005).
>   Among the oldest documented traded spices.
> - **Bible (Old Testament, ~1500-1000 BCE):** Cinnamon mentioned
>   as a sacred temple ingredient in Exodus 30:23; also in
>   Proverbs and Song of Solomon.
> - **Roman Empire:** Cinnamon (Latin *cinnamomum*) was among the
>   most expensive spices, traded via Indian Ocean and Red Sea
>   routes. Pliny the Elder describes cinnamon trade extensively.
>   Roman writers couldn't agree on the source — some thought it
>   came from Africa (where it was traded but didn't grow);
>   the actual Sri Lankan origin was secret of Arab traders.
> - **~1000 CE:** Arab traders maintained mystery about
>   cinnamon's origin to keep trade margins; competitor Indians
>   knew the Sri Lankan source but kept it quiet.
> - **1505:** Portuguese arrive in Sri Lanka, secure cinnamon
>   monopoly; later transferred to Dutch (1640s) then British
>   (1796).
> - **17th-18th century:** Cinnamon was so valuable it shaped
>   colonial-era trade routes, contributing to European imperial
>   expansion in South Asia.
> - **20th century:** Cassia cinnamon's lower price gradually
>   replaces Ceylon in mass-market commercial cinnamon; "true
>   cinnamon" Ceylon becomes a specialty product.
> - **2003:** Khan et al. *Diabetes Care* trial shows cinnamon
>   reduces fasting glucose 18-29% in type 2 diabetics — sparks
>   modern wellness interest in cinnamon supplementation.
> - **2008-2010:** EFSA publishes coumarin safety guidance,
>   shifting public awareness toward Ceylon for daily/therapeutic
>   use.
> `verified`

**Cultural roles:**

> - **Egyptian:** Embalming and religious offerings (~2000 BCE).
> - **Hebrew/Biblical:** Temple incense and anointing oil (Exodus
>   30:23-25); among the most sacred spices.
> - **Roman/Greek:** Religious offerings, perfumery, medicine,
>   cooking. Status symbol due to extreme expense.
> - **Chinese (TCM):** Cassia cinnamon (*Ròu Guì*, "cinnamon
>   bark") is among the 50 fundamental TCM herbs. Strong Yang-
>   warming, used for cold-deficient conditions.
> - **Ayurvedic:** *Twak* — used for digestion, cold conditions,
>   respiratory issues. Component in chai masala.
> - **Persian/Middle Eastern:** Featured in *advieh* and *baharat*
>   spice blends; widely used in rice dishes and stews.
> - **European medieval:** Mulled wine (*hippocras*), spice
>   cookies (*lebkuchen*, *speculaas*), apple preparations.
> - **Mexican:** Specifically Ceylon cinnamon (*canela*) is
>   traditional; stronger preference for Ceylon than in US/EU
>   markets. Mexican hot chocolate, Mexican coffee, *atole*.
> - **Modern Western:** Apple pie, French toast, cinnamon rolls,
>   Pumpkin Spice, breakfast cereals.
> `verified`

**The blood sugar evidence:**

> Cinnamon's modern wellness profile is largely built on its blood
> glucose effects. Key findings:
>
> - **Khan 2003 (*Diabetes Care*):** Foundational RCT — 60 T2D
>   patients given 1g, 3g, or 6g cinnamon daily for 40 days
>   showed 18-29% fasting glucose reduction across all doses;
>   triglycerides reduced 23-30%; LDL reduced 7-27%. (This is
>   *Cinnamomum cassia*; Ceylon less studied.)
> - **Akilen 2012 meta-analysis:** Significant decreases in HbA1c
>   and fasting glucose
> - **Allen 2013 meta-analysis (10 RCTs):** Cinnamon significantly
>   reduces fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, LDL, and
>   triglycerides
> - **Mechanism:** Cinnamaldehyde and procyanidin polyphenols
>   appear to enhance insulin sensitivity, slow gastric emptying,
>   and reduce intestinal carbohydrate absorption
>
> **Caveat:** Most positive trials use cassia at therapeutic doses
> (1-6g/day for 8-12 weeks), where cassia's coumarin becomes a
> safety concern. Ceylon-specific evidence is more limited but
> growing — recent Ceylon RCTs (PLOS One 2025) suggest comparable
> blood sugar effects with much better safety profile. (Multiple
> sources; Khan 2003)

**TCM framing:**

> Cassia cinnamon (*Ròu Guì*, 肉桂) is one of the 50 fundamental
> TCM herbs. Classified as warming and sweet-acrid, entering the
> Kidney, Spleen, and Heart meridians. Used for:
>
> - Warming the kidneys; supporting Yang (especially in elderly
>   or chronically cold patients)
> - Warming the middle (digestive center)
> - Activating blood circulation
> - Used in classic formulas like Gui Zhi Tang and Liu Wei Di
>   Huang Wan variations
>
> Different cuts of cinnamon bark have different specific TCM
> uses — *Gui Zhi* (cinnamon twig) is a separate medicinal from
> *Ròu Guì* (cinnamon bark). `verified`

**Ayurvedic framing:**

> *Twak* (cinnamon) is a key warming spice in Ayurveda.
> Reduces *kapha* and *vata*; can aggravate *pitta* in excess.
> Used for digestive complaints, cold conditions, respiratory
> congestion. Featured in chai masala and many digestive
> preparations. `verified`

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **Ceylon vs. Cassia as separate ingredients?** This is the
   most important question. The two species differ enough
   chemically (cinnamaldehyde concentration, coumarin) and in
   safety profile that treating them as one ingredient
   somewhat misrepresents the situation. Three options:

   - Keep one `cinnamon` entry with documentation flagging
     the distinction (current state)
   - Split into `cinnamon-ceylon` and `cinnamon-cassia` —
     more precise but adds catalog complexity
   - Add `cinnamon-ceylon` as a premium variant; keep
     `cinnamon` as cassia (the commercial default)

   Recommended: documentation approach for now; revisit if
   user demand for premium-tier distinction emerges.

2. **Coumarin risk communication.** The risk is real for daily
   high-dose cassia use but minimal for occasional culinary
   use. User-facing copy should communicate this proportionally
   — neither dismissing the safety issue nor catastrophizing
   it. The honest framing: "cassia is fine in food, Ceylon is
   safer for daily tea."

3. **Tea-strength vs. clinical-strength dosing.** Most cinnamon
   blood-sugar trials use 1-6g/day for weeks. A typical cup of
   cinnamon tea delivers ~0.5-1g cinnamon. Therapeutic effects
   scale with dose; tea-strength sits at the lower end of
   clinically-tested ranges.

4. **Pairing recommendations should surface chai context.**
   Cinnamon's most globally-significant tea use is in masala
   chai — combined with cardamom, ginger, cloves, black pepper
   over black tea + milk. Future Herbanium pairing logic
   should recognize this clustering.

5. **The "warming spice" calibration.** Ginger 5, cinnamon 4,
   turmeric 3 represents a clean spread. Future warming spices
   (cardamom, cloves, black pepper, allspice) will need to fit
   within or extend this scale.

6. **Apple-cinnamon pairing has cultural weight.** Across
   Western, Eastern European, and increasingly global contexts,
   apple-cinnamon is a deeply familiar pairing. Worth flagging
   for blend recommendations even though apple is not currently
   a Herbanium ingredient.

7. **Mexican Ceylon preference.** Mexican culinary tradition
   strongly prefers Ceylon (canela) over cassia. This makes
   "Mexican-style cinnamon" effectively a Ceylon synonym — a
   cultural detail that may matter for sourcing recommendations.

## Addendum — the register is `comfort`, not `soothing` (2026-08-03)

§5 files this ingredient's affective claim under `soothing`, but the
gloss describes warm relaxation rather than any action on tissue:

> | soothing | 2 | The warming-sweet combination soothes; especially when combined with milk (chai, golden milk) |

`soothing` is the **demulcent** register here — mucilage or tannin
acting on irritated surfaces, as in licorice's throat coat, linden's
mucilage, sage's gargle tradition. This one credits the warming-sweet
combination and milk, not cinnamon acting on anything. It is the
feeling of a chai, which is comfort.

Transcribed as `comfort` at the same strength. The evidence is
unchanged; the word is corrected.

## Addendum — prose claim audit (2026-08-23)

**The coumarin limit has a name, and the doc did not carry it.** §7
covers the cassia/Ceylon coumarin difference thoroughly — ~1% by
weight in cassia, about 25 mg in a teaspoon, against ~0.004% in
Ceylon. What it never named was the body behind the guidance the
facts list cites: the German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment
(Bundesinstitut für Risikobewertung, BfR), whose tolerable daily
intake for coumarin is 0.1 mg per kg of body weight — around 6 mg for
a 60 kg adult, which a single teaspoon of cassia exceeds several times
over. The claim was sound; the attribution was floating. `verified`
