## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The caffeinated holly leaf of South America — Argentina's
> national drink, the social ritual of Uruguay, the morning
> companion of Paraguayan farmers, and the increasingly visible
> alternative to coffee for Western drinkers seeking sustained
> energy without the crash.

**Short description:**

> Yerba mate is the dried leaves (and small stems) of *Ilex
> paraguariensis*, an evergreen tree of the holly family native
> to the subtropical forests of South America — primarily
> northern Argentina, Paraguay, southern Brazil, and Uruguay.
> The Indigenous Guaraní people first cultivated and consumed
> mate prior to European contact, considering the plant sacred
> and using it as a stimulant, social drink, and spiritual
> medicine. Spanish Jesuits commercialized cultivation in the
> 16th-17th centuries, and the drink spread throughout the
> Spanish colonial Americas. Today, mate is the national drink
> of Argentina (declared by law in 2013) and Uruguay, with
> Argentinians consuming an average of ~14 pounds of dried mate
> per person annually. Drunk by an estimated 30+ million people
> daily in South America. The traditional preparation is
> communal: dried leaves are packed into a hollowed gourd
> (*mate*, *guampa*, or *cuia*), hot water (70-85°C, never
> boiling) is added, and the infusion is sipped through a metal
> straw (*bombilla*) with a filter at the bottom. The same
> gourd and straw are passed around a circle, refilled by a
> designated server (*cebador*). Pharmacologically distinct
> from tea: contains caffeine alongside theobromine and
> theophylline (the same xanthine alkaloid family but in
> different proportions than coffee or tea), producing a
> sustained energy without the spike-and-crash characteristic
> of coffee.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- earthy
- grassy
- herbaceous
- bitter
- slightly smoky

**Aroma notes:**

> Distinctive earthy-vegetal character that doesn't resemble
> tea or coffee. The dominant notes are dried grass, hay, and
> green vegetable, with bitter undertones. Traditional
> Argentine-Uruguayan style is *con palo* (with stems) producing
> a gentler, more rounded profile. Brazilian *erva-mate* tends
> to be ground finer and brewed with less stem. Some processing
> styles use wood-fire drying which imparts a smoky character
> reminiscent of lapsang souchong — the *barbacuá* (smoke-dried)
> processing especially.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Substantial but not heavy. The bitterness is genuine and
> distinguishes mate from gentler herbal infusions. A slight
> astringency from chlorogenic acid and tannins. The first
> sip from a fresh gourd (*lavar el mate*) is often the most
> bitter; subsequent refills mellow as the leaves wash out.

**Basic tastes:**

> - `bitter` (3-4) — defining quality; characteristic
> - `astringent` (3) — significant
> - `earthy` (3) — vegetal-ground character
> - `sweet` (0-1) — minimal natural sweetness; sugar often
>   added in some traditions
> - `umami` (1-2) — slight savory undertone

> **Cultural note on flavor expectations:** Mate is meant to
> be bitter. Traditional consumers don't add sugar or
> sweeteners (though Argentine *mate dulce* and Brazilian
> *chimarrão* sometimes include sugar). Western newcomers
> often find unfamiliar bitterness off-putting initially;
> taste typically adjusts within a few weeks of regular
> consumption.

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [70, 85] | traditional, ref-mate-wikipedia | **Critical: never use boiling water.** The 70-85°C range is so culturally entrenched that some Argentine electric kettles have a "mate" setting at this range. Boiling water makes the cup harshly bitter |
| **time range (seconds)** | [60, 300] | traditional | Minimal steeping — the bombilla is inserted, water is poured, drink begins immediately. The "infusion" continues across multiple refills (10+ refills typical from one gourd-fill) |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 30-50 (per single fill); cumulative across refills can reach 80-120mg | ref-pinch-of-health | **First caffeinated herbal in catalog.** Lower per-cup than coffee (~95-200mg) but typical sessions involve multiple refills |
| **dose** | Gourd 2/3 full of dried mate (~30-50g of leaves for a multi-refill session) per session | traditional | The gourd is the unit, not the cup. A single session may produce 6-12+ "cups" of refilled liquid from one mate-fill |

> **The traditional preparation is fundamentally social, not
> individual:**
>
> 1. Fill the gourd (*mate*, *guampa*, *cuia*) about 2/3 with
>    dried yerba mate
> 2. Cover the opening with a hand and shake/tilt to settle
>    fines and create a slope of leaves on one side
> 3. Insert the bombilla into the empty/wetted side
> 4. Add cool water first (about 1-2cm depth) to wet the leaves
>    without scalding them — preserves flavor across many
>    refills
> 5. Add hot water (70-85°C) to the same side
> 6. The *cebador* (server) drinks the first fill — it's often
>    the harshest and quality-checks for the group
> 7. Pass the gourd to the next drinker, who drinks the entire
>    fill, returns the gourd to the cebador, who refills with
>    hot water and passes to the next person clockwise
> 8. Continue refilling until the mate is *lavado* (washed
>    out) — typically 10+ refills
>
> Important social rules:
> - Don't say "thank you" until you're finished — saying
>   *gracias* signals you're done and want to leave the
>   rotation
> - Don't move or touch the bombilla — it stays in place,
>   placed by the cebador
> - The mate is shared communally — same gourd, same straw
>   passed around
>
> **Modern individual preparation:** Single-serve mate in tea
> bags or metal infusers is common in non-traditional contexts
> (commercial cafes, Western consumers). This is more like tea
> brewing — pour hot water over leaves in a cup, steep 3-5
> minutes, drink. Loses the cultural depth but remains
> pharmacologically equivalent.

> **Cold preparation — *Tereré*:**
>
> Particularly popular in Paraguay and northeastern Argentina
> in summer: same gourd-and-bombilla preparation but with cold
> or iced water (sometimes with citrus, herbs, or flavorings
> added). *Tereré* is essentially required summer drinking in
> Asunción. Less caffeine extraction at cold temperatures but
> similarly social. `verified`

> **Vocabulary stress test — `energy` 4 from third mechanism:**
>
> Three ingredients now sit at `energy` 4:
> - **Assam:** Pure caffeine volume + thearubigins
> - **Matcha:** Concentrated caffeine + L-theanine synergy
> - **Yerba mate:** Caffeine + theobromine + theophylline triad
>
> Different mechanisms producing convergent functional effect.
> The vocabulary handles this gracefully — same `energy 4`
> rating, with character distinguished through other effects
> (matcha's `focus 5 + calm 3`; yerba mate's `focus 3 + digestive 2`;
> assam's `focus 3 + warming 4`).
>
> **Vocabulary stress test — Principle #16 implementation:**
>
> Yerba mate is the catalog's first caffeinated herbal. The
> simple resolution: `category: herbal`, `caffeine: 30-50`. No
> new category needed. User-facing filters can treat caffeine
> as orthogonal to category. This validates Principle #16's
> approach.
>
> **The "energy without crash" claim:**
>
> Widely repeated in Western marketing; partially validated by
> the xanthine alkaloid combination. Caffeine alone produces
> sharp peak-and-crash; theobromine has slower onset and
> longer half-life; theophylline contributes additional smooth
> stimulation. The combined kinetic profile genuinely produces
> a more sustained energy curve than caffeine alone. However,
> "no crash" overstates — heavy mate consumption can still
> produce caffeine withdrawal effects when stopped suddenly.
> `attested`.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**Caffeine considerations:**

> - **Caffeine sensitivity:** Single cups are mild; traditional
>   sessions can deliver substantial caffeine. Sensitive users
>   should start with single-cup preparations
> - **Pregnancy:** Standard caffeine guidance applies (under
>   200mg/day). Multiple refill sessions exceed this allowance
> - **Anxiety disorders:** Without L-theanine moderation, mate
>   can produce more anxiety than matcha at equivalent caffeine
> - **Sleep:** Avoid mate within 6 hours of bedtime for caffeine-
>   sensitive sleepers

**The hot mate / esophageal cancer association:**

> A genuine and well-documented concern. Multiple
> epidemiological studies have linked **very hot mate
> consumption** (above 65-70°C) with increased esophageal cancer
> risk in heavy traditional drinkers. The mechanism appears to
> be thermal damage to esophageal tissue from drinking very hot
> liquids regularly, not the mate itself. The IARC (International
> Agency for Research on Cancer) classifies "very hot beverages"
> (above 65°C) as Group 2A "probably carcinogenic" — this
> applies to coffee, tea, and other hot drinks too, but mate
> consumers traditionally drink hotter than tea drinkers.
>
> **Practical guidance:** Drink mate at the traditional 70-80°C
> range (the lower end), not above. This preserves both safety
> and traditional flavor. `established` (epidemiological
> evidence is solid)

**Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) concerns:**

> Some traditional processing (especially *barbacuá* — wood-fire
> drying) introduces polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (similar
> to lapsang souchong's smoke processing). Heavy consumption of
> heavily-smoked mate has been associated with elevated cancer
> risks in some studies. Modern commercial mate is increasingly
> air-dried or uses controlled smoke to minimize PAH exposure.
> Worth disclosing for users seeking smoke-dried varieties.
> `attested`

**Known interactions:**

> - **MAOIs:** Theoretical concern with monoamine oxidase
>   inhibitors due to xanthine alkaloid interactions; mostly
>   theoretical. `folk` to `attested`
> - **Stimulant medications:** Caffeine + theobromine + theophylline
>   adds to prescribed stimulants. Awareness rather than
>   contraindication. `attested`
> - **Anticoagulants:** Some pre-clinical evidence of mild
>   antiplatelet activity from chlorogenic acid; modest
>   clinical concern. `folk`

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"Mate causes cancer"** — Misframing of the hot-temperature
>   mechanism. Mate at appropriate temperature (70-80°C) does
>   not appear to be inherently carcinogenic. `folk`
> - **"Mate cures everything"** — Cultural exuberance about a
>   beloved drink; specific traditional medicinal claims are
>   modest. `folk`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origin:**

> *Ilex paraguariensis* is native to the subtropical forests
> of southern Brazil, northeastern Argentina, eastern Paraguay,
> and northern Uruguay. Grows as an evergreen tree reaching
> 8-15 meters in wild conditions; cultivated forms are pruned
> shorter for harvesting. Female plants tend to have lower
> caffeine and milder flavor than male plants. Most commercial
> production today is in Brazil (~58% of world production —
> 513,000+ tonnes in 2012), Argentina (~32%), and Paraguay
> (~10%). `verified`

**Historical timeline:**

> - **Pre-Columbian period:** The Indigenous Guaraní people
>   (whose territory covered present-day Paraguay, parts of
>   Argentina, southern Brazil, and Uruguay) cultivated and
>   consumed *ka'ay* ("herb water"). Considered sacred — used
>   for spiritual rituals, social bonding, and as stimulant
>   for long journeys. The Tupí people in adjacent regions
>   also used yerba mate. `verified`
> - **16th century Spanish colonial contact:** Spanish settlers
>   initially viewed mate consumption with suspicion (the
>   communal sharing seemed primitive) but quickly adopted
>   the practice. The Spanish word *mate* derives from
>   Quechua *mati*, meaning "vessel" — referring to the
>   gourd. `verified`
> - **17th-18th century Jesuit cultivation:** Jesuit missions
>   in Paraguay began commercial cultivation of mate (the
>   first systematic agricultural production), making mate
>   available beyond its native range. The drink spread
>   throughout Spanish colonial South America. The Jesuits
>   were eventually expelled in 1767, but commercial
>   cultivation continued. `verified`
> - **18th-19th century gauchos:** The legendary cowboys of
>   the Argentine and Uruguayan pampas adopted mate as
>   essential gear — the bombilla and gourd became iconic
>   gaucho equipment, drunk during long days driving cattle.
>   Mate became deeply identified with rural Argentine
>   national identity. `verified`
> - **19th century European interest:** Some attempt to
>   commercialize mate in Europe; never achieved coffee/tea
>   level adoption. Charles Darwin sampled mate during the
>   Beagle voyage (1830s) and wrote favorably about it.
>   `attested`
> - **20th century mass consumption:** Mate becomes an
>   everyday Argentine drink consumed across class lines —
>   from elite politicians to rural workers. Industry
>   consolidates with major brands (Cruz de Malta, Taragüí,
>   La Merced, Rosamonte). `verified`
> - **2013:** Argentine law (Ley 26.871) declares yerba mate
>   the national drink of Argentina. `verified`
> - **2015 onward:** National Mate Day declared in Argentina
>   (November 30 — birthday of Andrés Guacurarí, indigenous
>   leader who promoted mate cultivation). `verified`
> - **2020s Western boom:** Western health and wellness
>   markets discover mate; it appears in energy drinks
>   (Guayaki, Mate Maté, etc.), specialty cafes, and
>   wellness blogs. Argentine and Uruguayan immigrants share
>   the cultural practice with new audiences globally.

**Cultural significance:**

> Mate is far more than a beverage in its core cultural areas:
>
> - **National identity:** Argentina, Uruguay, and Paraguay all
>   regard mate as foundational to national identity. Argentina
>   formally declared it the national drink (2013); Uruguay
>   has perhaps even higher per-capita consumption rates.
> - **Social ritual:** The shared gourd represents trust,
>   equality, and hospitality. Refusing offered mate or treating
>   it disrespectfully is read as rejecting the social bond.
> - **Daily life structure:** In Argentina, mate punctuates the
>   day — morning mate at home, *mate cocido* (boiled mate, often
>   with milk for children) for breakfast, mate breaks at
>   work, mate at family gatherings.
> - **Communal continuity:** Multiple generations drink from
>   the same gourd in family settings. The gourd itself becomes
>   personally meaningful and is often kept for life.
> - **George Gaylord Simpson's 1930 Patagonia observation:**
>   Mate is the "center of social life — perhaps of life
>   itself."
> - **Modern variation:** Younger Argentines and urban
>   professionals increasingly drink mate from individual
>   thermoses (*termo*) at work — adapting tradition to
>   modern professional life without losing the cultural form.
>
> User-facing copy should honor this depth without trivializing
> it as just "South American tea." `verified`

**Modern clinical evidence:**

> Yerba mate has accumulated solid clinical research:
>
> - **Antioxidant activity:** Mate contains higher polyphenol
>   content than green tea by some measures. Strong in vitro
>   antioxidant capacity. `established`
> - **Cardiovascular markers:** Multiple studies show modest
>   improvements in lipid profiles (LDL reduction) with regular
>   mate consumption. `attested`
> - **Glucose regulation:** Some evidence for mild glucose-
>   lowering effects in diabetic and pre-diabetic individuals.
>   `attested`
> - **Weight management:** Modest effects on satiety and
>   metabolism — likely mediated by caffeine. `attested`
> - **Cognitive function:** Caffeine + theobromine alertness
>   effects well-documented. `established`
> - **Cancer concerns:** Hot-temperature consumption associated
>   with esophageal cancer (see safety section); appropriate-
>   temperature consumption appears safe. `established` for
>   the temperature mechanism
>
> Compared to coffee or tea, mate has mid-tier clinical
> evidence — strong on caffeine-mechanism effects, modest on
> specific metabolic benefits.

**Botanical note: the "mateine" question:**

> Some traditional and marketing literature claims mate contains
> "mateine" — a compound supposedly distinct from caffeine that
> produces gentler effects. **This is botanically incorrect.**
> "Mateine" is chemically identical to caffeine; the apparent
> difference comes from the accompanying alkaloids (theobromine
> and theophylline) modifying the kinetic profile, not from
> a different stimulant compound. Honest copy should clarify
> this without making mate seem either deceptive or
> inferior to coffee. `verified` (the chemical identity);
> `attested` (the kinetic difference).

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **Schema reconciliation needed.** Like matcha and turmeric,
   yerba mate doesn't fit the standard steeping mechanic. The
   traditional preparation is a continuous multi-refill session,
   not a single-cup steep. May need a `preparation_pattern`
   field.

2. **Principle #16 validated.** First caffeinated herbal in
   catalog. The simple resolution (`category: herbal`,
   `caffeine: 30-50`) works cleanly. No new category needed;
   user-facing filters orthogonal.

3. **The "mateine" myth.** Worth honestly correcting in user-
   facing copy without making it seem like mate is deceptive.
   Frame as: "Mate contains caffeine alongside theobromine and
   theophylline (similar to chocolate's stimulant profile),
   producing a different felt experience than pure caffeine —
   but the caffeine is real caffeine."

4. **The hot-mate cancer concern.** Real and well-documented;
   specific to very hot drinking temperatures. Standard
   guidance: drink at 70-80°C (the cooler end of traditional
   range), not above. Worth disclosing in safety copy.

5. **Cultural depth challenge.** Like matcha and tea ceremony,
   mate has profound cultural depth that user-facing copy
   should engage honestly. Not a generic "South American tea"
   — it's a defining cultural ritual of multiple countries.

6. **The communal vs. individual tension.** Traditional mate
   is fundamentally communal; modern Western consumption is
   typically individual. Worth surfacing both modes in copy
   without dismissing either.

7. **Pairing recommendations.** Mate is traditionally consumed
   alone or with bombillas of additional herbs (mint, lemongrass,
   citrus peel — *yuyos*). Modern blending: pairs with
   lemongrass, mint, citrus, ginger. Less commonly blended with
   true tea or florals.

8. **Roasted vs. unroasted (*verde* vs. *tostada*) distinction.**
   Argentine commercial mate is typically *verde* (unroasted,
   green); Brazilian *erva-mate* often *tostada* (roasted) for
   *chimarrão* preparation. Different sensory profiles. Could
   become a future split per Principle #18 if catalog complexity
   grows.

9. **Ethical sourcing considerations.** Major Argentine and
   Brazilian mate producers have varying labor and environmental
   practices. Worth flagging as future topic for sourcing
   guidance — similar to coffee or chocolate ethics.

10. **The energy-drink association.** Many Western users
    encounter mate first through energy drinks (Guayaki,
    Mate Maté, Yerba Mate Bros, etc.). User-facing copy
    should engage this gateway honestly — these are mate, but
    typically with added caffeine, sugar, or other ingredients.

## Addendum — `uplifting` transcribed, `cooling` cannot be (2026-08-02)

Same shape as matcha, and the same cause: **§6 here models three
preparations, not a temperature gradient** — the traditional gourd
(*mate*/*chimarrão*), the Western tea bag, and *tereré*.

### `uplifting` — transcribed

Prescribed at all three preparations and rated 2 in §5 as "the bright
social register; mate is associated culturally with conversation,
friendship, daytime energy." General across preparations, so it
translates. Shipped at 2, dropping to 1 at the lightest cup — §6b, the
tea-bag preparation, is the one the doc rates at 1, and the 70°C
sample is its closest analogue.

### `cooling` — not transcribable

Prescribed **only** for tereré, and §5 is explicit that this is not a
general property: *"cooling | | Not primary; slight in tereré (cold
preparation)."*

Tereré is served at **5–10°C**. The coldest sample in the shipped
profile is 70°C. There is no cup in this profile that tereré
corresponds to, and putting `cooling` on a 70°C sample would assert
that hot mate is cooling — which is the opposite of what §5 says.

The honest answer is that the app cannot currently express a cold
preparation of a hot-brewed ingredient. That's a real limitation of
the profile shape, worth knowing about, and better recorded than
papered over. If cold-brew preparations ever get their own axis,
tereré is the first thing to model.
