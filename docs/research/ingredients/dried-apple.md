## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> Soft, honeyed sweetness from dried fruit — the universal sweetener of herbal blends, no sugar required.

**Short description:**

> Dried apple appears in nearly every commercial herbal
> blend that wants natural sweetness without added sugar
> or stevia. The drying process concentrates the fruit's
> natural sugars (mostly fructose and sorbitol) and softens
> the malic acid into a gentler tart-sweet register. In
> infusion, dried apple gives up its sugars over a long
> steep, contributing body and sweetness more than aroma —
> it's a backbone fruit, not a top-note fruit. Traditional
> European herbalism uses apple in *Bircher* muesli infusions
> and the German *Apfeltee* (with cinnamon and a clove or
> two). The fruit's pectin contributes a slight body to the
> cup. Apple pairs cleanly with almost everything: chamomile,
> hibiscus, rose, cinnamon, vanilla, rooibos, fennel. Its
> "soothing" character is more about texture and sweetness
> than any active compound — the cup feels gentler with
> apple in it. No caffeine, no stimulants, no
> contraindications at tea levels.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- apple
- sweet
- fruity
- honey

**Aroma notes:**

> Soft, honeyed apple — closer to baked-apple or
> apple-cider than fresh apple. Reads as sweetness more
> than as a distinct fruit aroma in a blend.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Medium body. Pectin and sugars give the cup a slight
> roundness — apple-heavy blends drink "fuller" than
> spice or floral blends without sugar.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**headsUp field:**

> No notable cautions at tea levels. Dried apple is food.

**Dosage cautions:**

> A heaping teaspoon per cup is generous. More just
> makes the cup sweeter — there's no bitter ceiling to
> worry about.

**Not a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> "Apple pectin detoxifies" — pectin is a soluble fiber,
> not a detoxifier. The amount in tea is negligible
> compared to eating an apple.

## 10. Facts for the Steep screen

| # | Type | Confidence | Fact | Source |
|---|------|-----------|------|--------|
| 1 | culture | verified | German *Apfeltee* — dried apple, cinnamon, and a clove or two — has been a winter staple in Bavarian and Austrian households for centuries. The name predates the word "tea" entering German; it was originally just *Apfelsud*, "apple decoction." | ref-2 |
| 2 | fact | verified | Drying concentrates apple's sugars: a fresh apple is about 10% sugar by weight, while dried apple is 50–60%. This is why dried apple sweetens a cup that fresh apple slices wouldn't touch. | ref-1 |
| 3 | fact | verified | Apple pectin gives a tea cup a noticeable roundness — pectin is a soluble fiber that thickens liquid slightly, the same property used to set jam. This is what makes apple-heavy blends drink "fuller" than spice-only blends. | ref-1 |
| 4 | history | verified | The Bircher-Benner clinic in 1900s Zürich used apple infusions and apple-based muesli as part of a raw-food diet protocol — the muesli is named for its founder, who originally prescribed it to tuberculosis patients. | ref-3 |
| 5 | fact | verified | Apple is one of the few infusion ingredients that has no over-pull ceiling worth worrying about. Long steeps don't make it bitter — they just make it sweeter. | ref-1 |
