## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The gentler garden mint — sweeter and milder than peppermint,
> carvone-driven instead of menthol-driven, the basis of
> Moroccan mint tea and most Middle Eastern "mint tea," and
> uniquely supported by clinical RCT evidence for anti-androgenic
> effects in women with PCOS.

**Short description:**

> Spearmint is the leaves of *Mentha spicata*, a perennial
> herb in the mint family native to Europe and Western Asia,
> cultivated globally for over 2,000 years. The dominant
> aromatic compound is **carvone** (50-80% of essential oil),
> not menthol — this is the key chemical distinction from
> peppermint and explains spearmint's sweeter, gentler character.
> Used heavily in Middle Eastern, North African, Mediterranean,
> and increasingly Western cuisines and beverages. Most "mint
> tea" served across the Arab world (e.g., Moroccan mint tea
> with green gunpowder) is spearmint, not peppermint. Modern
> clinical research has identified an unusual specialty: spearmint
> tea has documented anti-androgenic effects (lowers free
> testosterone) in women with PCOS and hirsutism — the only
> common herbal tea with rigorous human RCT evidence for this
> specific application.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- mint
- sweet
- bright
- gentle
- slightly herbal

**Aroma notes:**

> Cleaner, sweeter, and less assertive than peppermint. The
> dominant compound carvone produces a "minty" character
> without the cooling-sharpness of menthol. Subtle floral and
> herbaceous undertones from limonene, linalool, and minor
> compounds. Spearmint smells distinctly "garden-fresh" — closer
> to garden mint just-picked than to commercial mint products.
> Fresh spearmint dramatically more aromatic than dried; quality
> dried spearmint should still smell brightly minty when crushed.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Gentler than peppermint — provides a mild cooling sensation
> without the assertive cold-sharp register that menthol
> creates. The cup feels "sweet and rounded" rather than
> "fresh and stimulating." No bitterness at standard brewing.
> Slight astringency at extended steeps.

**Basic tastes:**

> - `aromatic` (3) — minty character
> - `sweet` (2) — natural sweetness more pronounced than
>   peppermint
> - `bitter` (0-1) — minimal
> - `astringent` (1-2) — slight
> - `cool` (2-3) — present but milder than peppermint's
>   menthol cooling

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [85, 95] | traditional | Slightly below boil to preserve volatile aromatics; carvone is heat-sensitive |
| **time range (seconds)** | [180, 480] | traditional | 3-8 min; spearmint releases flavor relatively quickly. Forgiving of longer steeps |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 0 | universal | Caffeine-free |
| **dose** | 1-2 tsp dried leaves OR 5-7 fresh leaves per 250ml | traditional | Generous use is fine; spearmint doesn't dominate at typical tea doses |

> **Fresh vs. dried:**
> - **Fresh leaves:** Significantly more aromatic; the
>   "Moroccan mint tea" experience uses fresh leaves
>   traditionally. Carvone is at peak intensity.
> - **Dried leaves:** Standard commercial form; loses some
>   bright top notes but retains essential character. Quality
>   dried spearmint should still smell vividly minty.
>
> For users with garden access, fresh spearmint tea is
> meaningfully different from dried — worth surfacing this
> distinction.

## 5. Effects — felt

| Effect | Strength (0-5) | Notes |
|--------|---------------|-------|
| cooling | 3 | **Primary effect.** Sits intentionally below peppermint (4) — spearmint's carvone produces a milder cooling than menthol. Joins lemongrass, hibiscus, and gunpowder at this strength. Different mechanism from peppermint or lemongrass — three distinct "cool 3" pathways |
| digestive | 3 | Strong but milder than peppermint (4). Same general mechanism (smooth muscle relaxation) but lower potency. Traditional Mediterranean and Middle Eastern after-meal use |
| uplifting | 2 | The bright minty top notes lift mood; a "garden-fresh" cheerful register |
| calm | 2 | Mild calming via mint family GABAergic activity |
| soothing | 1 | Gentle |
| focus | 1 | Mild alertness from aromatic stimulation |
| energy | | Not primary |
| sleepy | | Not primary — spearmint is brighter than soporific |
| warming | | Opposite direction |
| grounding | | Not primary |

> **Vocabulary stress test — `cooling` axis at 3 (third entry):**
>
> Spearmint joins lemongrass and gunpowder/hibiscus at `cooling`
> 3. Each represents a different cooling mechanism:
> - **Spearmint:** Carvone-mediated mild cooling
> - **Lemongrass:** Citral-mediated bright-cool
> - **Gunpowder:** Catechin-mediated astringent cooling
> - **Hibiscus:** Acid-cooling refreshment
>
> The vocabulary handles the convergent effect ("cooling 3")
> without distinguishing the underlying chemistry. Peppermint
> (menthol → TRPM8 receptor activation) legitimately holds the
> ceiling at 4.
>
> **The PCOS / anti-androgenic specialty:**
>
> Spearmint is the only catalog ingredient with documented
> anti-androgenic effects in human RCTs (Akdogan 2007, Grant
> 2010). Carvone and rosmarinic acid appear to inhibit androgen
> production via CYP21A2 and other pathways. Effect: 2 cups/day
> of spearmint tea significantly lowers free testosterone in
> women with PCOS and hirsutism. This doesn't map to current
> vocabulary (no "hormonal regulation" effect category) — it's
> a specialty application worth surfacing in copy without
> needing vocabulary representation.

### 6d. Moroccan mint tea — the iconic preparation

> The most globally-known spearmint preparation is Moroccan
> mint tea (*atay bi naʽnaʽ* or *thé à la menthe*):
>
> 1. Boil water in *berrad* (Moroccan teapot)
> 2. Add gunpowder green tea (~1 tbsp per 4 cups)
> 3. Add fresh spearmint leaves (generous handful)
> 4. Add sugar (significant amount — 4-6 tsp per pot
>    traditionally; lighter for modern preferences)
> 5. Steep 3-5 minutes
> 6. Pour from height (the "rising" pour) to aerate and
>    create foam on top
> 7. Often poured 3 times, with second and third pours
>    progressively sweeter and stronger
>
> This preparation combines spearmint's gentle cooling with
> gunpowder green tea's astringent-energy character, balanced
> by significant sweetness. The cultural framing is hospitality
> and prolonged conversation; serving Moroccan mint tea is a
> social ritual across Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Mauritania,
> and Western Sahara. UNESCO recognized Maghrebi tea drinking
> as part of intangible cultural heritage.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**Known interactions:**

> - **Anti-androgen medications:** Spearmint's anti-androgenic
>   effect could theoretically add to spironolactone or other
>   anti-androgens; clinically not significant at tea doses
>   but worth noting for women on PCOS medication. `attested`
> - **Hormone-sensitive conditions:** The anti-androgenic
>   activity is generally welcome in PCOS but worth knowing
>   for users with other hormone-sensitive conditions where
>   the effect direction matters (e.g., low-testosterone
>   conditions in men). `attested`
> - **GERD/heartburn:** Like peppermint, spearmint can relax
>   the lower esophageal sphincter, potentially worsening
>   GERD in some individuals. Effect is milder than peppermint.
>   `attested`

**Pregnancy:**

> Generally considered safe in normal culinary/tea amounts.
> Limited human safety data for high-dose supplementation in
> pregnancy. Tea-strength use is universally consumed across
> Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, and North African pregnancy
> traditions without notable safety concerns. `attested`

**Men's health note:**

> Spearmint's anti-androgenic effect is generally consumed
> intentionally by women with PCOS but could theoretically
> reduce testosterone in men consuming high quantities daily.
> Casual consumption (occasional cups of mint tea) is unlikely
> to produce clinically significant effects in men. Daily
> heavy consumption (3+ cups/day for weeks) might warrant
> awareness. `folk` to `attested`

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"Spearmint cures PCOS"** — Spearmint can help manage
>   specific PCOS symptoms (hirsutism, androgen-driven acne)
>   but doesn't address PCOS comprehensively. `folk`
> - **"Spearmint causes infertility"** — Misframing of anti-
>   androgenic research; no evidence of fertility impairment
>   from culinary/tea consumption. `folk`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origin:**

> *Mentha spicata* is native to Europe and Western Asia,
> cultivated and naturalized across temperate regions globally.
> The species name *spicata* refers to the spiked
> inflorescence. One of the most easily-grown garden herbs;
> spreads readily via underground rhizomes (often considered
> mildly invasive in gardens). `verified`

**Historical use:**

> - **Ancient Greek and Roman:** Pliny the Elder described
>   mint as "stirring up gluttony"; ancient Greeks rubbed
>   mint on banquet tables to encourage appetite. Spearmint
>   is the species most commonly identified in classical
>   Mediterranean texts. `verified`
> - **Roman period:** Carried throughout Roman territories;
>   established in Britain and across Europe via Roman
>   cultivation. `verified`
> - **Medieval European herbalism:** Continuous use; gerard's
>   Herbal (1597) noted mint "rejoices the heart of man" —
>   echoing the lemon balm "merry heart" tradition. `verified`
> - **Islamic Golden Age:** Spearmint becomes central to
>   Middle Eastern hospitality and culinary tradition;
>   Avicenna documents it. The Arabic *naʽnaʽ* tradition
>   spreads with Islamic civilization. `verified`
> - **18th-19th century Maghreb:** Moroccan mint tea
>   tradition develops, combining Chinese gunpowder green
>   tea (introduced via European trade) with native
>   spearmint, sugar (also via trade), and traditional
>   *berrad* serving vessels. Becomes definitive Maghrebi
>   cultural ritual. `verified`
> - **Modern era:** Maintains universal culinary use; modern
>   research identifies anti-androgenic effects (2007 onward).
>   Increasingly featured in PCOS-management copy.
>   `verified`

**The Moroccan mint tea cultural significance:**

> Moroccan mint tea (and broader Maghrebi mint tea tradition)
> is a defining cultural ritual:
>
> - **Hospitality:** Refusing offered mint tea is considered
>   rude in many Maghrebi contexts.
> - **Social bonding:** Tea is shared across age, gender, and
>   class lines; the hours-long social gathering around tea
>   is foundational to community life.
> - **Three pour tradition:** The first pour is described as
>   "bitter as life"; the second as "strong as love"; the
>   third as "sweet as death" — reflecting the deepening
>   character across pours.
> - **The rising pour:** Pouring from height (~30cm above
>   cups) aerates the tea, creates foam, and is essentially
>   a performance ritual that demonstrates skill and
>   hospitality.
> - **Berber influence:** The tradition predates and is
>   shaped by Indigenous Berber (Amazigh) culture as well as
>   Arabic influence.
>
> UNESCO inscribed "Maghreb tea-drinking culture" on the
> Intangible Cultural Heritage list (2024 — Algeria, Morocco,
> Mauritania, Tunisia joint nomination). Worth surfacing in
> user-facing copy. `verified`

**Modern clinical evidence:**

> Spearmint has accumulated specific clinical research,
> notably for anti-androgenic effects:
>
> - **Akdogan 2007:** First clinical trial showing spearmint
>   tea (2 cups/day) significantly lowered free testosterone
>   in women with hirsutism over 5 days
> - **Grant 2010 (RCT, Phytotherapy Research):** Randomized
>   controlled trial of 42 women with PCOS-related hirsutism;
>   spearmint tea twice daily for 30 days significantly
>   reduced free and total testosterone, increased LH and
>   FSH. Subjective hirsutism scores improved; objective
>   hirsutism scores didn't change in 30 days (insufficient
>   time for hair cycle changes)
> - **Subsequent animal and human studies:** Multiple
>   confirmations of anti-androgenic effects; emerging
>   mechanism via CYP21A2 inhibition and androgen pathway
>   modulation
> - **Cognitive function:** Some studies suggest spearmint
>   extract supports memory and cognitive function (separate
>   research line from PCOS work)
> - **Antimicrobial:** Robust in vitro evidence
>
> The anti-androgenic specialty distinguishes spearmint from
> most herbal teas — it's one of the few traditional remedies
> with rigorous human RCT evidence for a specific clinical
> application beyond general digestive/calming claims.
> `established` (anti-androgenic effect in PCOS)

**TCM framing:**

> Adopted into TCM via Mediterranean trade. Generally
> classified as cooling, with affinity for the Liver and
> Stomach meridians. Used for cooling internal heat, moving
> qi, calming irritability. Less central than peppermint in
> classical TCM. `attested`

**Ayurvedic framing:**

> *Pudina* in Ayurveda generally refers to mint family broadly;
> spearmint specifically is *Pudina ki garden mint*. Cooling,
> sweet-pungent, *pitta*-pacifying. Used for digestion,
> respiratory complaints, and as cooling food in summer.
> `attested`

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **The PCOS specialty is unusual.** Spearmint is one of very
   few catalog ingredients with a rigorous RCT-supported
   specific clinical application beyond general categories.
   Worth surfacing in copy for users seeking PCOS-management
   support, while being honest that effects take weeks to
   manifest visibly.

2. **The anti-androgenic effect doesn't map to vocabulary.**
   This is a real pharmacological effect (testosterone
   reduction) without a clean vocabulary mapping. Captured
   through copy rather than effects array. Adding to v2
   vocabulary consideration list (along with anti-inflammatory,
   stress-buffering, anesthetic).

3. **The cooling axis at 3 holds.** Spearmint, lemongrass,
   gunpowder, and hibiscus all sit at `cooling` 3 via different
   mechanisms. Peppermint at 4 holds the ceiling.

4. **The Moroccan mint tea connection.** Spearmint + gunpowder
   green tea is a culturally and chemically harmonious pairing
   that user-facing blend logic should recognize.

5. **The men's-health note.** Spearmint's anti-androgenic
   effect is generally welcome in women with PCOS but
   theoretically concerning for men consuming heavy daily
   amounts. Tea drinking at typical levels is unlikely to
   cause clinically significant effects in men, but worth
   honest disclosure.

6. **Pairing recommendations.** Spearmint pairs especially
   well with: gunpowder green tea (Moroccan), lemongrass
   (mint-citrus brightness), chamomile (gentle evening),
   ginger (warming-cooling balance), peppermint (mint-on-mint
   layering), rooibos (sweet-mint harmony), licorice (when
   added — not currently in catalog).

7. **Mint family clustering with peppermint and lemon balm.**
   All three (spearmint, peppermint, lemon balm) are Lamiaceae
   family with overlapping but distinct profiles. Vocabulary
   captures the differences cleanly: peppermint cooling 4 +
   digestive 4; spearmint cooling 3 + digestive 3; lemon balm
   calm 4 + sleepy 3.

8. **Fresh vs. dried distinction matters.** Like lemongrass and
   lemon balm, spearmint volatiles degrade. Quality fresh
   spearmint tea is meaningfully superior to dried; worth
   surfacing for users with garden access.

## Addendum — `energy` corrected to `focus` (2026-08-02)

An audit found `energy` (1–2) shipped with nothing in this document
behind it. The literature does support a real cognitive effect, but it
is explicitly **stimulant-free** — so the claim was in the wrong
register rather than simply absent.

> *The attention-enhancing effects of spearmint extract supplementation
> in healthy men and women: a randomized, double-blind,
> placebo-controlled, parallel trial.*
> https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0271531718308431
>
> 142 healthy adults aged 18–50, 900 mg/day spearmint extract over 90
> days: improvements in **attention and working memory**. A separate
> trial found rosmarinic-acid-rich spearmint extract improved working
> memory and spatial reasoning in adults with age-associated memory
> impairment.

The active fraction is rosmarinic acid, and spearmint is caffeine-free;
sources describe it as a stimulant-free alternative to coffee for
focus. Recorded as `focus` at the same strengths — the evidence
supports the effect, only the register was wrong.
