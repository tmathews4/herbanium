## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The cured orchid fruit — sweet, creamy, universally comforting,
> with a history that reads like an adventure novel: Aztec
> chocolate, Spanish conquistadors, a stingless Mexican bee, a
> 12-year-old enslaved boy, and a colonial monopoly that lasted
> 300 years.

**Short description:**

> Vanilla is the cured fruit of orchids in the *Vanilla* genus,
> primarily *V. planifolia*. Native to the Gulf Coast of Mexico,
> first cultivated by the Totonac people, and adopted by the
> Aztecs who used it to flavor their *xocolatl* chocolate drink.
> Introduced to Europe by Hernán Cortés in the 16th century.
> For nearly 300 years, Mexico held a natural monopoly on
> vanilla production because the orchid required a specific
> Mexican pollinator that didn't exist elsewhere. In 1841, a
> 12-year-old enslaved boy named Edmond Albius on Réunion Island
> discovered hand-pollination — breaking the monopoly and
> enabling global production. Today Madagascar produces 70-80%
> of world supply. Vanilla is the second most expensive spice
> after saffron, driven by extreme labor-intensity (hand-
> pollination, 8-9 month pod development, months of curing).

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- sweet
- creamy
- warm
- floral
- complex

**Aroma notes:**

> The archetypal "sweet comfort" aroma — vanilla has been called
> one of the most universally-loved flavors in human culture.
> Dominant compound is vanillin (responsible for ~85% of vanilla's
> character), but real vanilla contains 200+ additional aromatic
> compounds that give it depth and complexity synthetic vanillin
> cannot replicate. Notes include: cream, honey, butter, light
> caramel, subtle floral, occasional fruity or smoky undertones
> (varies by origin — Madagascar Bourbon is creamy, Tahitian is
> fruity-floral, Mexican is bold/smoky).
>
> The characteristic vanilla aroma develops only during the
> months-long curing process — fresh green vanilla pods are
> almost odorless. The curing is essentially controlled
> enzymatic-fermentation that liberates vanillin from its
> glucoside precursor (glucovanillin) and produces the complex
> secondary aromatics.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Creates perceived creaminess and body in drinks even without
> actual dairy — vanilla's aromatic complexity reads as "full"
> or "rounded" on the palate. Combined with milk or other fat,
> the creaminess compounds dramatically. No astringency; no
> bitterness; genuinely smooth. Slight warming quality in
> extended sipping.

**Basic tastes:**

> - `sweet` (3) — perceived sweetness (vanilla doesn't contain
>   significant sugar but creates strong sweet-expectation
>   response, possibly via cultural association with sweet
>   contexts)
> - `bitter` (0-1) — minimal; whole pod tea can have slight
>   edge from pod itself
> - `umami` (1) — subtle; the complex aromatic depth produces
>   an almost-savory undertone sometimes

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [85, 95] | traditional | Lower than most spices — vanilla aromatics are volatile and high heat pushes them off |
| **time range (seconds)** | [600, 900] | traditional | 10-15 min for whole split pod. Vanilla extracts slowly; longer infusion produces fuller flavor. Can steep 30+ min for very full extraction |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 0 | universal | Caffeine-free |
| **dose** | 1/2 pod split lengthwise per 250ml | traditional | A single pod usually sufficient for 1-2 cups; split lengthwise exposes seeds |

> **Form matters significantly:**
>
> - **Whole split pod:** Cleanest flavor for tea use; slower
>   extraction; can be re-steeped 2-3 times
> - **Vanilla extract (alcohol-based):** Culinary form; 1/4-1/2
>   tsp works for a cup but can add alcohol edge
> - **Vanilla paste:** Seed-and-bean blend; concentrated but
>   expensive
> - **Synthetic vanillin / imitation vanilla extract:** Single-
>   compound flavoring from lignin or petroleum precursors;
>   lacks the 200+ secondary aromatics that give real vanilla
>   its complexity. Functional for baking; notably flat for
>   tea.

## 5. Effects — felt

| Effect | Strength (0-5) | Notes |
|--------|---------------|-------|
| soothing | 3 | **Primary effect.** Vanilla is culturally the "comfort flavor" across most of the world where it has cultural presence. Not a pharmacological effect in the way ginger's warming is — this is aromatic/psychological, but still real and reliable |
| calm | 2 | Aromatherapy tradition reports vanilla as calming; some limited research on vanillin's mild anxiolytic effects in animal models |
| uplifting | 2 | The sweet-aromatic register lifts mood without being bright/stimulating — more "cozy uplift" than "energetic uplift" |
| warming | 1 | Mild perceived warming from aromatic character; not physiological TRPV1-style warming like ginger |
| energy | | Not primary |
| focus | | Not primary |
| sleepy | 1 | Slight drowsy-comfort quality in strong preparations (linked to the "milk + vanilla before bed" tradition) |
| cooling | | Opposite direction |
| grounding | | Not primary — vanilla is soft rather than grounded |
| digestive | | Not primary |

> **Vocabulary observation — aromatic effects flag:**
>
> Vanilla is the first catalog ingredient where effects are
> driven **primarily by aromatic/psychological response rather
> than pharmacological action**. Ginger genuinely activates TRPV1
> receptors; chamomile's apigenin binds GABA receptors; gyokuro's
> L-theanine crosses the blood-brain barrier. Vanilla's "effects"
> — soothing, calm, uplifting — are culturally mediated and
> aromatic rather than directly pharmacological.
>
> This doesn't make them less real. Aromatic/psychological
> effects are genuine experiences that users report consistently.
> But it's a different mechanism category worth flagging for the
> vocabulary framework. Future ingredients with similar profiles
> might include rose (which also leans aromatic/psychological
> despite some pharmacology) and certain fruity herbals.
>
> Current approach: use the existing vocabulary with reduced
> confidence markers. All vanilla effect claims are marked
> `attested` rather than `established`, reflecting that the
> cultural-aromatic mechanism is real but softer than
> pharmacological claims.

### 6d. Variety-specific notes

> The three main commercial vanilla species produce meaningfully
> different teas:
>
> - ***V. planifolia* (Bourbon/Madagascar):** Creamy, sweet,
>   buttery. The "default" vanilla most drinkers expect.
> - ***V. tahitensis* (Tahitian):** More floral, more fruity
>   (notes of cherry, anise). Delicate; works well for cold
>   infusions and pastry; can be less robust in strong tea
>   preparations.
> - ***V. pompona* (Caribbean):** Less common; more intense,
>   almost hazelnut character.
>
> Most commercial "vanilla" in Herbanium user context is going
> to be Bourbon/Madagascar. Tahitian is a boutique/premium
> purchase. Pompona is rare.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**Known interactions:**

> None significant at culinary/tea doses. Vanilla is among the
> safest ingredients in the catalog — centuries of widespread
> use, no major drug interactions, no pregnancy concerns at
> normal doses. `established`

**Pregnancy:**

> Culinary vanilla (in baking, beverages) is universally safe
> in pregnancy. Vanilla extract contains alcohol (typically
> 35%+ ABV) so alcohol-avoidant pregnancies may prefer whole
> pods or vanilla paste. `verified`

**Synthetic vs. natural vanillin:**

> Vanillin (the dominant vanilla compound) is chemically
> identical whether extracted from vanilla pods or synthesized
> from lignin/petroleum precursors. Safety profile is
> equivalent; sensory profile is NOT equivalent (real vanilla
> has 200+ additional compounds). For tea applications, real
> vanilla is significantly better; for baking, synthetic is
> often adequate at much lower cost. `established`

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"Vanilla is addictive"** — Comfort-food cultural pattern,
>   not pharmacological addiction. `folk`
> - **"Vanilla is aphrodisiac"** — Long-standing folk claim
>   across cultures; no clinical evidence. `folk`
> - **"All vanilla extract contains alcohol"** — Most does;
>   some commercial formulations use glycerin or propylene
>   glycol for alcohol-free versions. Whole pod is inherently
>   alcohol-free. `verified`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origin — uniquely an orchid:**

> Vanilla is the only commercially significant orchid product.
> *Vanilla planifolia* is a climbing vine-orchid native to the
> humid lowland forests of Gulf Coast Mexico (Veracruz and
> surrounding regions) and parts of Central America. Pre-
> domestication range extended through the Caribbean and south
> to Ecuador. The plant is a sterile triploid in cultivated
> form (like bananas and seedless grapes), reproduced
> exclusively through cuttings. `verified`

**The pollination mystery — key to vanilla's history:**

> Vanilla orchid flowers open for only a single day. If not
> pollinated within that window, the flower wilts without
> producing a pod. In Mexico, specific native pollinators (long
> believed to be the Melipona bee, though recent research
> questions this singular attribution — orchid bees and
> hummingbirds also contribute) pollinate the flowers. These
> pollinators don't exist outside Mexico.
>
> This biological constraint created a natural Mexican monopoly
> on vanilla production for 300 years (~1520-1841). Spanish
> conquistadors and later European colonial powers repeatedly
> tried to establish vanilla production elsewhere (Réunion,
> Madagascar, various Caribbean islands), with vines growing
> and flowering but almost never fruiting. `verified`

**Historical timeline:**

> - **Pre-Columbian (12th century or earlier):** Totonac people
>   of the Gulf Coast of Mexico cultivate wild vanilla.
>   Considered sacred; used in ceremonial drinks and medicine.
>   Totonac mythology: vanilla orchid born from the blood of
>   Princess Xanat, forbidden to marry her mortal lover.
>   `attested`
> - **1427:** Aztecs conquer the Totonac. Vanilla incorporated
>   into Aztec *xocoatl* chocolate drink (combined with cacao,
>   honey, and spices). Served exclusively to nobility; used
>   as currency in Aztec tribute. `verified`
> - **1519:** Hernán Cortés arrives in Tenochtitlan; introduced
>   to vanilla-chocolate drink by Emperor Moctezuma II. Cortés
>   brings vanilla back to Spain. `verified`
> - **16th century:** Spanish aristocracy develops taste for
>   vanilla-chocolate; vanilla spreads through European courts.
>   Mexico remains sole producer. `verified`
> - **1819:** French entrepreneurs ship vanilla fruits to
>   Réunion and Mauritius hoping to break Mexican monopoly.
>   Vines grow but fail to fruit consistently. `verified`
> - **1837:** Belgian botanist Charles François Antoine Morren
>   discovers vanilla's pollination requirement; develops
>   hand-pollination method, but it's commercially impractical
>   at his technique level. `verified`
> - **1841:** Edmond Albius, a 12-year-old enslaved boy on
>   Réunion Island (then called Île Bourbon), develops
>   practical hand-pollination using a wooden splinter to lift
>   the rostellum and press pollen onto the stigma. The
>   technique is essentially unchanged today. Albius is
>   initially credited falsely by his French botanist owner
>   Jean Michel Claude Richard; by end of 20th century Albius
>   is recognized as the true discoverer. `verified`
> - **1848:** Albius freed with abolition of slavery in French
>   colonies. Dies in poverty 1880 despite the economic
>   transformation his discovery enabled. `verified`
> - **Post-1841:** Vanilla cultivation spreads to Indian Ocean
>   islands (Réunion, Comoros, Madagascar), later Tahiti,
>   Indonesia, Uganda. Madagascar becomes dominant producer by
>   early 20th century. `verified`
> - **1889:** Mexican vanilla exports exceed 70,000 kilograms;
>   briefly remain a world leader before Madagascar overtakes
>   them. `verified`
> - **20th century:** Madagascar establishes ~70-80% global
>   market share. The SAVA region of northeast Madagascar
>   becomes the vanilla-producing heartland. `verified`
> - **21st century:** Vanilla prices become extremely volatile
>   due to Madagascar cyclones, Malagasy political instability,
>   and rising demand. Prices peaked over $600/kg in 2018,
>   briefly making vanilla more expensive than silver.
>   `verified`

**Cultural roles:**

> - **Mexican/Mesoamerican:** Sacred origin plant; Totonac
>   cultural pride; *xocolatl* flavoring; modern Mexican bakery
>   tradition preserves bold, smoky-profile Mexican vanilla
>   production.
> - **European (post-16th century):** Flavoring aristocratic
>   chocolate; Vienna's *Sachertorte* tradition; French pastry
>   craft; Italian gelato.
> - **American:** Backbone flavor of American baking (cookies,
>   cakes, ice cream); vanilla ice cream as "default" flavor;
>   vanilla extract in the home spice rack.
> - **South Asian:** Limited historical use; increasing in
>   modern baking and tea blends.
> - **Global modern:** Vanilla is perhaps the most
>   universally-loved flavor in existence — featured in
>   desserts, perfumes, aromatherapy, beverages across almost
>   every modern cuisine.
> `verified`

**The Edmond Albius legacy:**

> Albius's discovery is one of the most economically consequential
> innovations of the 19th century — enabling a multi-billion-
> dollar global industry — made by a 12-year-old enslaved boy
> who received neither financial reward nor lasting recognition
> during his lifetime. A statue of Albius now stands on Réunion;
> the Madagascar vanilla industry that grew from his discovery
> supports hundreds of thousands of smallholder farmers. The
> story is both a triumph of ingenuity and an uncomfortable
> reminder of how colonial-era labor systems distributed the
> rewards of innovation. Worth engaging honestly with rather
> than skating past in user-facing copy. `verified`

**TCM and Ayurvedic framing:**

> Vanilla is not part of classical Chinese or Ayurvedic materia
> medica — it's a New World plant that arrived too late to
> integrate into those traditional systems before the systems
> ossified. Modern TCM/Ayurvedic practitioners sometimes
> include vanilla in formulations, generally framing it as
> sweet-warming and mood-supporting, but without classical
> textual authority. `attested`

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **Aromatic effects vs. pharmacological effects.** Vanilla is
   the first catalog ingredient where effects are primarily
   aromatic/psychological rather than pharmacological. All
   vanilla effect claims marked `attested` rather than
   `established` to reflect this distinction. Vocabulary v2
   might benefit from a meta-tag distinguishing "aromatic/
   cultural" from "pharmacological" effects — or at least
   documenting this category explicitly.

2. **Real vs. synthetic vanilla.** Commercial "vanilla flavor"
   is usually synthetic vanillin (from lignin or petroleum
   precursors), lacking the 200+ secondary aromatics of real
   vanilla. For tea applications, real vanilla is meaningfully
   superior; for baking, synthetic is often adequate. Worth
   surfacing in user guidance without being snobbish.

3. **Regional variety matters more than for most ingredients.**
   Madagascar/Bourbon, Tahitian, and Mexican vanillas are
   sensorially quite different. Tahitian especially is a
   distinct product (different species entirely). Future
   catalog expansion could consider variants.

4. **Whole pod vs. extract vs. paste vs. powder.** Different
   forms produce different tea experiences. Whole pod is the
   traditional and highest-quality tea form but has a short
   shelf-life once split. Worth documenting in user guidance.

5. **Price volatility is real.** Madagascar cyclones and
   political instability cause vanilla prices to swing
   dramatically. User-facing copy should be aware that
   "vanilla is expensive" is more variable than "saffron is
   expensive" — vanilla has briefly been more expensive than
   silver in 2018.

6. **Historical narrative includes slavery.** The Edmond Albius
   story is central to vanilla's global history and cannot be
   honestly told without engaging the fact that a 12-year-old
   enslaved boy made the innovation that transformed the
   industry without receiving just compensation. User-facing
   copy should handle this honestly rather than skipping to
   "Madagascar vanilla is great" without the context.

7. **Chai and golden-milk cluster pairings.** Vanilla pairs
   exceptionally well with cinnamon, cardamom, and the existing
   warming spice cluster. Also classic pairings: vanilla + rose,
   vanilla + chamomile, vanilla + black tea. Worth documenting
   for future blend-recommendation logic.

8. **Orchid-family uniqueness.** Vanilla being the only
   commercial orchid is a fun fact that could be surfaced in
   user-facing copy — distinguishes it sensorially from the
   "other spices" category.

## Addendum — `digestive` kept, sourced as tradition (2026-08-02)

An audit flagged `digestive` as unsourced. §5 rates `soothing` 3,
`calm` 2, `uplifting` 2, `warming` 1 and `sleepy` 1 — no digestive
row. The traditional record does carry one.

> Vanilla has traditionally been used as an aphrodisiac, **carminative**,
> antipyretic and stimulant, and in the treatment of fever, spasm,
> dysmenorrhea and hysteria. — Natural Products Database monograph,
> https://www.drugs.com/npp/vanilla.html

Carminative is the relevant word: the same register as fennel's
anethole and the catalogue's other after-meal ingredients, arrived at
by tradition rather than by trial.

**The limit is the same one §5 already sets for vanilla's primary
effect.** That row says outright that vanilla's `soothing` is "not a
pharmacological effect in the way ginger's warming is — this is
aromatic/psychological, but still real and reliable." The digestive
claim sits in the same category: documented, cross-cultural,
mechanistically unproven. **No controlled trial tests vanilla for
digestion**, and it should not be presented as though one did. It
belongs at the 2–3 it ships rather than anywhere near fennel's 4.

## Addendum — the register is `comfort`, not `soothing` (2026-08-03)

§5 rates this ingredient's primary affective claim under `soothing`,
but its own gloss describes something else:

> | soothing | 3 | **Primary effect.** Vanilla is culturally the "comfort flavor" across most of the world where it has cultural presence. Not a pharmacological effect in the way ginger's warming is — this is aromatic/psychological, but still real and reliable |

`soothing` in this catalogue is the **demulcent** register — the
materia medica action where mucilage or tannin acts on irritated
tissue. Licorice's throat coat, linden's mucilage, sage's gargle
tradition. It is a claim about the body's surfaces.

The row says "comfort flavor" and then says outright that the effect is
aromatic and psychological rather than pharmacological. That is the
definition of the comfort register and the opposite of a demulcent
claim.

Transcribed as `comfort` at the same strength. Nothing about the
evidence changes; the word does.
