## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The bright tropical grass that smells like lemon — kitchen
> staple from Bangkok to Kingston, caffeine-free, and one of the
> few traditional herbal teas that genuinely tastes refreshing
> rather than medicinal.

**Short description:**

> Lemongrass is the leaves and stalks of *Cymbopogon citratus*,
> a perennial tropical grass native to South Asia and Maritime
> Southeast Asia. Cultivated for at least 2,000 years; now grown
> across the tropics with major production in India (the world's
> largest essential oil producer), Thailand, Guatemala, Brazil,
> and parts of Africa. The characteristic bright citrus aroma
> comes from citral — an aldehyde that makes up 65-85% of
> lemongrass essential oil — supplemented by myrcene, geraniol,
> citronellol, and limonene. Used heavily in Thai, Vietnamese,
> Cambodian, and Indonesian cuisine; brewed as a tea across
> Southeast Asia, the Caribbean (where it's called "fever grass"),
> Latin America (*hierba de limón*), and increasingly in Western
> herbal blends. Caffeine-free at the cellular level — not
> "decaffeinated" but inherently lacking caffeine.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- lemon
- bright
- fresh
- grassy
- slightly sweet

**Aroma notes:**

> Vivid lemon-citrus character, brighter and cleaner than lemon
> peel itself — described accurately as "lime zest meets grass"
> rather than "lawn clippings." Citral is the defining compound
> (a mixture of two isomers, geranial and neral), with geraniol
> contributing rose-floral undertones. Quality lemongrass should
> register strongly aromatic when crushed; faded product is
> dusty and one-note. Fresh lemongrass (just-cut) is dramatically
> more aromatic than dried — citral degrades within 6-12 months.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Light and refreshing; the cup tastes "clean" without the
> heaviness of many herbal infusions. Slight grassy bite that
> distinguishes from pure lemon flavor. No astringency or
> bitterness at standard brewing — lemongrass is forgiving of
> longer steeps. A subtle warming-after-cooling sensation
> develops in extended sipping (citral has both cool and
> slight-warm characters).

**Basic tastes:**

> - `aromatic-citrus` (4) — defining quality
> - `sweet` (1-2) — slight natural sweetness
> - `bitter` (0-1) — minimal even with extended steeping
> - `astringent` (1) — slight
> - `cool` (2) — present but milder than peppermint or spearmint

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [90, 100] | traditional | Full or near-boil; citral and other aromatics extract well at high temperature |
| **time range (seconds)** | [240, 480] | traditional | 4-8 min; lemongrass releases flavor relatively quickly compared to root materials. Forgiving of longer steeps |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 0 | universal | **Inherently caffeine-free**, not decaffeinated. *Cymbopogon* species don't produce caffeine at the cellular level |
| **dose** | 1 tbsp dried leaves OR 1 fresh stalk (lower bulb portion, lightly bruised) per 250ml | traditional | Generous use is fine; lemongrass doesn't dominate other flavors at typical tea doses |

> **Stalk vs. leaves:**
> - **Fresh stalk** (lower bulb portion, the part used in Thai
>   curries): Most aromatic, denser flavor; needs to be bruised
>   or split lengthwise to release oils. Best for traditional
>   Southeast Asian-style preparations.
> - **Dried leaves** (cut and sifted, the standard tea form):
>   More accessible; lighter aromatic profile; suitable for
>   blending. Quality varies significantly with freshness.
> - **Powdered:** Usually inferior; loses too much aromatic
>   character.

> **Vocabulary stress test — `cooling` axis at 3:**
>
> Lemongrass at `cooling` 3 fits cleanly between the peppermint
> ceiling (4) and the gentler cooling herbs (cardamom, gyokuro,

> via carvone), lemongrass and spearmint represent two distinct

### 6d. Cultural preparations

> Lemongrass appears in distinctly different beverage contexts
> across cultures:
>
> - **Thai/Southeast Asian:** Often combined with pandan,
>   ginger, and palm sugar in chilled tea preparations. Also
>   the aromatic backbone of *tom yum* and *tom kha gai* soups
>   (not technically a tea but related culinary use).
> - **Caribbean ("fever grass"):** Steeped strong and used for
>   cold/flu/fever symptoms. Often combined with ginger and
>   honey.
> - **Brazilian folk medicine:** Brewed for anxiety
>   (*capim-cidrão*), traditionally consumed in evening.
> - **West African:** Used in similar fever/cold contexts;
>   sometimes combined with hibiscus.
> - **Modern Western herbal:** Common base in "lemon ginger"
>   blends; pairs well with green tea, ginger, hibiscus,
>   chamomile.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**Known interactions:**

> - **Anticoagulants:** Some pre-clinical evidence of mild
>   antiplatelet activity; theoretical concern at high doses.
>   Not clinically significant at tea doses. `folk`
> - **Diabetes medications:** Limited evidence of mild
>   hypoglycemic effects; theoretical additive risk. `folk`
> - **Photosensitivity:** Topical lemongrass essential oil can
>   cause photosensitivity in some individuals; not a concern
>   for tea consumption. `attested`

**Pregnancy:**

> Generally considered safe in normal culinary/tea amounts.
> Some traditional caution due to potential mild emmenagogue
> (menstruation-promoting) effects, though clinical evidence is
> minimal at tea doses. Avoid concentrated essential oil and
> high-dose supplements during pregnancy. `attested`

**FDA GRAS status:**

> Lemongrass essential oil is on the FDA's Generally Recognized
> as Safe (GRAS) list for food use. The herb is widely consumed
> in cooking globally without significant safety concerns.
> `verified`

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"Lemongrass cures cancer"** — Pre-clinical anti-tumor
>   signals (citral, geraniol); no clinical translation.
>   Standard pattern. `folk`
> - **"Lemongrass repels mosquitoes when consumed"** —
>   Confusion with citronella (related species used for
>   topical insect repellent). Drinking lemongrass doesn't
>   produce repellent body odor. `folk`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origin:**

> *Cymbopogon citratus* is native to maritime Southeast Asia
> (Indonesia, Malaysia, Brunei, Philippines). *C. flexuosus*
> (East Indian lemongrass, used primarily for essential oil) is
> native to Cambodia, Vietnam, Laos, India, Sri Lanka, Myanmar,
> and Thailand. Both species are now naturalized across the
> tropics worldwide. The genus name *Cymbopogon* derives from
> Greek *kymbe* (boat) + *pogon* (beard) — referring to the
> boat-shaped spathes from which the hairy spikelets project.
> `verified`

**Historical use:**

> Lemongrass has been used in Asian traditional medicine and
> cuisine for at least 2,000 years. Cultural roles:
>
> - **South Asian (Ayurveda, Siddha):** Classified as
>   stimulant, sudorific (sweat-promoting), antiperiodic
>   (preventing recurrence of periodic fevers), anticatarrhal.
>   Used for fevers, colds, digestive complaints. `verified`
> - **Southeast Asian cuisines:** Foundational ingredient in
>   Thai (especially *tom yum*, *tom kha gai*), Vietnamese
>   (*pho*, marinades), Cambodian, Indonesian, Malaysian,
>   Filipino cooking. Used both fresh stalks and dried leaves.
> - **Chinese herbal medicine:** *Xiang mao* — used for damp-
>   heat conditions, summer heat. Less central than in
>   Southeast Asian or South Asian traditions.
> - **Caribbean tradition:** Known as "fever grass" (English
>   Caribbean) or *zagriyen* (Haitian Creole); brewed strong
>   for cold/flu/fever; central to Jamaican folk medicine.
> - **Brazilian folk medicine:** *Capim-santo* or *capim-cidrão*;
>   brewed for anxiety, insomnia, digestive complaints. Listed
>   in the Brazilian Pharmacopoeia as a phytomedicine.
> - **West African traditional medicine:** Various cultural uses
>   for fevers and digestive issues. Cultivated commercially.
> - **Samoan and Tongan tradition:** Crushed leaves used as
>   topical remedy for oral infections (*moegalo*, *moengālō*).
>
> The pattern across cultures is striking: lemongrass is used
> for similar things (fever, cold, digestion, mild anxiety)
> across cultures with no shared history of practice — a sign
> of consistent direct experience rather than transmitted
> medical traditions. `verified`

**Trade and global spread:**

> - **Pre-15th century:** Use confined to native range
>   (South/Southeast Asia) and adjacent regions reached by
>   maritime trade.
> - **15th-16th centuries:** European colonial trade routes
>   spread lemongrass to the Caribbean and South America.
>   Successful naturalization in Caribbean island climates.
> - **19th-20th centuries:** Major essential oil production
>   centers established in India, Brazil, Guatemala, Madagascar.
> - **Post-WWI:** *C. citratus* introduced to Madagascar,
>   South America, and Central America. Naturalization
>   completes globally.
> - **Late 20th century onward:** Rapid adoption in Western
>   herbal tea markets; standard component in "lemon" herbal
>   blends.
> `verified`

**Modern clinical evidence:**

> Lemongrass has accumulated some clinical research, though
> less than the heavily-studied catalog ingredients:
>
> - **Gastroprotective effects:** 2012 animal study (Tognolini
>   et al. context) showed lemongrass essential oil protected
>   stomachs from ethanol- and aspirin-induced damage.
>   Suggests potential as protective against NSAID gastropathy.
> - **Anti-anxiety (small human study):** Brazilian folk use
>   tested in a small RCT showed no statistically significant
>   anxiolytic effect, though aromatherapy studies of
>   lemongrass essential oil have shown anxiety reduction in
>   stressful situations.
> - **Antimicrobial:** Robust in vitro evidence against
>   bacterial, fungal, and protozoal pathogens. Citral and
>   geraniol have well-characterized antimicrobial activity.
> - **Oral thrush in HIV/AIDS:** Small randomized trial showed
>   lemongrass infusion as effective as standard treatment for
>   oral thrush — interesting low-cost application.
> - **Cytoprotective/antioxidant/anti-inflammatory:** Multiple
>   in vitro studies show consistent antioxidant and anti-
>   inflammatory activity from citral and geraniol.
>
> Overall: stronger pre-clinical than clinical evidence base,
> but the breadth of traditional cross-cultural use is itself
> meaningful. `attested` to `established` depending on claim.

**TCM framing:**

> *Xiang mao* (lemongrass) was incorporated into Chinese
> medicinal practice via Southeast Asian trade. Classified as
> cooling, slightly bitter, with affinity for the Spleen and
> Lung meridians. Used for damp-heat conditions, summer heat,
> and minor digestive complaints. `attested`

**Ayurvedic framing:**

> *Bhustrina* in Sanskrit. Classification:
>
> - **Rasa:** *Tikta* (bitter) + *Katu* (pungent)
> - **Virya:** *Shita* (cooling)
> - **Vipaka:** *Katu*
> - **Dosha effect:** Pacifies *Pitta* and *Kapha*; mildly
>   aggravates *Vata* in excess
>
> Used for fevers, headaches, bronchitis, digestive complaints.
> Considered cooling in the Ayurvedic framework, consistent
> with Caribbean "fever grass" tradition. `verified`

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **Caffeine-free framing matters.** Many users assume herbal
   teas are inherently caffeine-free; lemongrass is genuinely
   so at the cellular level (vs. decaffeinated true tea, which
   still contains trace caffeine). Worth surfacing for caffeine-
   sensitive users.

2. **Cross-cultural medicinal pattern.** Lemongrass is used for
   similar things (fever, cold, digestion, anxiety) across
   cultures with no shared medical heritage. This convergent
   traditional pattern is unusual and meaningful — suggests
   real direct-experience efficacy rather than transmitted
   tradition.

3. **The cooling axis at 3.** Lemongrass and spearmint both sit
   at `cooling` 3 via different mechanisms (citral vs. carvone).
   Vocabulary handles this gracefully without distinguishing
   the chemistry.

4. **Variety distinction (citratus vs. flexuosus).** Most
   commercial lemongrass tea is *C. citratus*. *C. flexuosus*
   is mostly used for essential oil. Worth noting in research
   file but probably not user-facing.

5. **Pairing strengths.** Lemongrass pairs well with: ginger
   (universal), green tea (Japanese-Western fusion), hibiscus
   (Caribbean "sorrel" style), chamomile (gentle evening),
   spearmint (mint-citrus brightness), pandan (Southeast
   Asian).

6. **Brazilian *capim-santo* tradition.** Lemongrass is more
   central in Brazilian folk medicine than most Western users
   realize. Could be surfaced for international users
   appreciating the cultural depth.

7. **The bee-attraction note.** Beekeepers use lemongrass oil
   to attract bees (it mimics a Nasonov gland pheromone).
   Charming fact that could surface in user-facing copy.

## Addendum — `energy` removed (2026-08-02)

An audit found `energy` (2–3) shipped at every brew point with nothing
in this document behind it. A literature check found no support for a
stimulant or energising effect; the pharmacology runs the other way.

> Carlini et al., *Pharmacology of lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus
> Stapf) III: assessment of eventual toxic, hypnotic and anxiolytic
> effects on humans* — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2429120/
>
> Animal work reports sedative, anxiolytic and anticonvulsant effects.
> The human trial found lemongrass atoxic but with **no** hypnotic or
> anxiolytic effect: sleep induction, sleep quality, dream recall and
> reawakening were all unchanged versus placebo.

So the honest picture is neither stimulant nor sedative in humans.
`energy` removed and nothing added in its place — the remaining
cooling, digestive, uplifting and calm claims stand on their own
entries above.
