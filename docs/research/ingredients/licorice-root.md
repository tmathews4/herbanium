## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The harmonizing sweet root — TCM's universal formula
> ingredient (in nine out of ten classical Chinese formulas),
> ancient Egyptian medicine's pharaoh drink, and Western
> herbalism's throat-soother — with a real safety profile that
> requires honest disclosure (chronic high-dose use elevates
> blood pressure and depletes potassium via mineralocorticoid-
> like effect).

**Short description:**

> Licorice root is the dried root and underground stem of
> *Glycyrrhiza glabra* (the Mediterranean species used in
> Western herbalism and most candy/flavoring) and
> *G. uralensis* (the Chinese species used in TCM). Native to
> the Mediterranean basin, Western Asia, and parts of China,
> licorice is among the oldest documented medicinal plants —
> mentioned in Egyptian medical papyri (~3000 BCE), used in
> ancient Greek and Roman medicine, central to TCM since at
> least 25 CE, and continuously employed across Mediterranean,
> Persian, Indian, and East Asian medical traditions. The
> primary active compound is **glycyrrhizin** (also called
> glycyrrhizic acid or glycyrrhizinic acid), a triterpenoid
> saponin that is approximately **50 times sweeter than sugar**
> by weight. Glycyrrhizin is metabolized in the gut to
> glycyrrhetinic acid, which inhibits 11-β-hydroxysteroid
> dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2) — the enzyme that converts
> active cortisol to inactive cortisone in the kidney. This
> mechanism explains both licorice's medicinal properties
> (extending cortisol's anti-inflammatory effects) and its
> primary safety concern (mineralocorticoid-like effects causing
> sodium/water retention, potassium loss, and blood pressure
> elevation). In TCM, licorice is the universal "harmonizer" —
> appearing in nine out of ten classical formulas to moderate
> the harshness of other herbs and increase formula tolerability.
> Modern uses span throat soothing, cough suppression, gastric
> ulcer support (especially DGL — deglycyrrhizinated licorice),
> adrenal fatigue framing in alternative medicine, and
> antiviral/anti-inflammatory contexts.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- intensely sweet
- anise
- woody
- earthy
- slightly bitter

**Aroma notes:**

> Distinctive sweet-anise character that is unmistakable.
> Glycyrrhizin contributes the dominant sweetness; anethole
> and related compounds (similar to fennel and star anise)
> contribute the licorice-anise aromatic register. The flavor
> is so distinctive that "licorice" has become a flavor
> descriptor in its own right — used to describe other foods
> (fennel, anise, tarragon, certain wines) that share aromatic
> compounds. Quality dried licorice root has a strong,
> recognizable aroma even before brewing.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Heavy, syrupy sweetness even at modest brewing strengths.
> Slight woody/fibrous quality from the root material. Some
> astringency from polyphenols. The sweetness lingers
> persistently — licorice "stays" in the mouth far longer than
> most teas, which is part of the traditional "harmonizing"
> framing (it makes other flavors more palatable).

**Basic tastes:**

> - `sweet` (5) — defining; **the sweetest single ingredient
>   in the catalog by intrinsic chemistry** (50x sucrose by
>   weight at the molecular level)
> - `aromatic` (3) — the anise register
> - `bitter` (1) — slight, mainly in poorly-prepared
>   preparations
> - `astringent` (1) — modest
> - `umami` (1) — slight savory undertone

> **Sensory note:** Licorice root represents the catalog's
> sweetness ceiling. Even fennel (sweet 3) sits well below
> licorice's 5. The intensity is genuine, not metaphorical —
> a small amount of licorice transforms any blend's sweetness
> profile substantially.

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [95, 100] | traditional | Full or near-boil; root material requires aggressive extraction |
| **time range (seconds)** | [300, 900] | traditional, ref-tcm-herb | 5-15 min; longer extraction yields stronger sweetness and stronger glycyrrhizin content |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 0 | universal | Caffeine-free |
| **dose** | 1/2 - 1 tsp dried sliced or chipped root per 250ml | traditional | **Critical: less is more.** 1 tsp produces a strongly-sweet cup; 2 tsp can be cloyingly sweet and increases pseudoaldosteronism risk |

> **The blend-component reality:**
>
> Licorice root is **rarely consumed alone**. The intense
> sweetness and the safety profile both encourage use as a
> small fraction (5-15%) of a blend rather than as a primary
> ingredient. Traditional uses:
>
> - **TCM formulas:** Licorice typically 5-15% of a multi-herb
>   formula (Chrysalis Natural Medicine notes "approximately
>   five percent of a formula" as standard)
> - **Throat soothers:** Combined with marshmallow root,
>   slippery elm, ginger, lemon, honey
> - **Western herbal blends:** Licorice as sweet-anchor in
>   roasted root blends with chicory, dandelion root, burdock
> - **Cough syrups:** Component of many traditional and modern
>   cough preparations
>
> User-facing copy should set expectations: licorice tea
> typically means licorice-blend tea, not solo licorice. Solo
> licorice is intensely sweet and approaches dose limits quickly.

> **Critical dose ceiling:**
>
> The pharmacologically-relevant safety threshold is approximately
> **3 grams of dried root per day for more than 6 weeks**, or
> **100 mg of glycyrrhizin per day**. To translate to tea:
>
> - 1 tsp dried licorice root ≈ 2-3 grams
> - 1-2 cups per day of typical-strength licorice tea = 2-6 grams root
> - Daily consumption at 6+ grams approaches and exceeds
>   the long-term safety threshold
>
> **Practical guidance:** Occasional cups (a few times per
> week) are well within safe range. **Daily continuous use,
> especially of solo licorice tea or strong preparations,
> warrants caution and should not exceed 4-6 weeks without
> medical evaluation.** Users with hypertension, kidney
> disease, heart disease, or pregnancy should avoid
> meaningful licorice consumption entirely.

> **Vocabulary stress test — `soothing` 4 calibration:**
>
> Licorice joins rooibos and hojicha at `soothing` 4. Each
> reaches this level through different mechanisms:
> - **Rooibos:** Daily-tonic gentle support across body systems
> - **Hojicha:** Roasted gentle character; low caffeine
>   gentling effect
> - **Licorice:** Throat-soothing demulcent + cortisol-extending
>   anti-inflammatory mechanism
>
> The vocabulary captures the convergent functional effect
> (`soothing 4`) without distinguishing the mechanisms. User-
> facing copy can surface licorice's specific demulcent and
> throat-soothing role.
>
> **Vocabulary stress test — sweet basic taste at 5:**
>
> Most ingredients have sweet ratings of 0-3. Fennel reaches
> 3 via anethole. Licorice's 5 (via glycyrrhizin's 50x sucrose
> sweetness) is genuinely an outlier — and it justifies the
> sweet-axis ceiling at 5. Future ingredients are unlikely to
> exceed without something like stevia (which isn't on the
> catalog roadmap).
>
> **Vocabulary observation — the harmonizer effect:**
>
> Licorice's most distinctive role isn't a felt-effect at all
> — it's the way licorice modifies the experience of OTHER
> herbs in a blend. This "harmonizing" quality (TCM's *tiao
> he zhu yao*) doesn't map to any vocabulary effect; it's a
> blend-recommendation property. Worth surfacing in copy when
> licorice appears as a blend component.

### 6d. The honey-fried (蜜炙甘草 *zhi gancao*) TCM preparation

> Traditional Chinese medicine uses two forms of licorice root:
>
> - **Sheng gancao (生甘草, raw licorice):** Cooling and
>   harmonizing; clears heat; soothes spasm; clears toxins
> - **Zhi gancao (炙甘草, honey-fried licorice):** Warming and
>   tonifying; tonifies *qi*; warms digestion; specifically
>   used in formulas treating spleen and stomach qi deficiency
>
> The two forms are pharmacologically and traditionally
> distinct in TCM, though Western catalogs typically conflate
> them. For Herbanium purposes, "licorice root" means raw
> (sheng) licorice unless specifically indicating the honey-
> fried preparation. The distinction is interesting cultural
> depth but probably below threshold for catalog splitting per
> Principle #18.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**The pseudoaldosteronism syndrome — central safety concern:**

> Chronic or high-dose licorice consumption can cause
> **pseudoaldosteronism**: a condition resembling primary
> hyperaldosteronism, characterized by:
>
> - **Hypertension** (blood pressure elevation)
> - **Hypokalemia** (low potassium)
> - **Sodium and water retention** (edema)
> - **Metabolic alkalosis**
> - **Suppressed renin and aldosterone activity**
>
> Mechanism: Glycyrrhetinic acid (the metabolite of
> glycyrrhizin) inhibits 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase
> type 2 (11β-HSD2) in the kidneys. This enzyme normally
> converts cortisol (active glucocorticoid) to cortisone
> (inactive). When inhibited, cortisol accumulates in renal
> tubules and activates mineralocorticoid receptors —
> producing aldosterone-like effects despite no aldosterone
> elevation.
>
> **Threshold:** ≥3 g/day dried root or ≥100 mg/day
> glycyrrhizin for ≥6 weeks. Some sensitive individuals show
> effects at lower doses.
>
> **Risk factors that lower the threshold:**
> - Older age
> - Pre-existing hypertension
> - Hypokalemia or low-potassium diet
> - Concurrent diuretic use (especially loop or thiazide)
> - Concurrent corticosteroid use
> - Pregnancy
> - Hypoalbuminemia
> - Constipation (slows glycyrrhizin clearance)
>
> **Reversibility:** Effects fully reverse within weeks of
> discontinuation. Acute clinical management is straightforward
> for most cases.
>
> `established`

**Contraindications (avoid licorice entirely):**

> - **Hypertension** (existing or borderline) — even moderate
>   licorice consumption can elevate BP
> - **Heart disease, especially congestive heart failure** —
>   sodium/water retention worsens cardiac load
> - **Kidney disease** — impaired clearance increases risk
> - **Liver disease** — though licorice has some hepatoprotective
>   uses, advanced liver disease is a contraindication
> - **Pregnancy** — Finnish cohort studies suggest licorice
>   consumption during pregnancy associated with adverse
>   outcomes including preterm birth and child cognitive
>   effects (Strandberg 2002, Räikkönen 2017)
> - **Hypokalemia or potassium-wasting conditions**
> - **Edema**
>
> `established`

**Known interactions:**

> - **Thiazide and loop diuretics:** Additive potassium
>   depletion. Dangerous in some patients. `established`
> - **Cardiac glycosides (digoxin):** Hypokalemia from
>   licorice increases digoxin sensitivity, raising arrhythmia
>   risk. `established`
> - **Corticosteroids (prednisolone, hydrocortisone):**
>   Glycyrrhizin extends corticosteroid half-life;
>   theoretically beneficial in some contexts but unpredictable.
>   `attested`
> - **Spironolactone:** Licorice can blunt the antihypertensive
>   effect of spironolactone (mechanism conflict). `attested`
> - **Warfarin:** Some interaction reported; mixed evidence
>   on direction. `attested`
> - **MAOIs:** Avoid combination per traditional and modern
>   guidance. `folk` to `attested`
> - **Hormonal medications:** Licorice has complex effects on
>   estrogen and cortisol pathways; pregnancy and hormone-
>   sensitive condition concerns. `attested`

**The DGL alternative:**

> **Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL)** has had glycyrrhizin
> removed (typically <2-3% remaining), eliminating the
> pseudoaldosteronism risk. DGL retains many of the
> demulcent/soothing properties (relevant flavonoids and
> polysaccharides remain) but loses the deeper anti-inflammatory
> effects mediated by glycyrrhizin. DGL is the standard
> recommendation for users who want digestive/throat support
> without the BP/potassium risk.
>
> **Note:** DGL is typically a supplement form (chewable
> tablets), not a tea form. DGL is mostly outside catalog
> scope; mention it for users seeking safer alternatives.
> `verified`

**Pregnancy:**

> **Avoid licorice during pregnancy.** Multiple cohort studies
> from Finland (Strandberg 2002 onward; Räikkönen 2017
> follow-up) suggest associations between pregnancy licorice
> consumption and:
> - Preterm birth
> - Lower birth weight
> - Cognitive and behavioral effects in children at age 13
>
> The mechanism is thought to be glucocorticoid effects on
> fetal HPA axis development. The Finnish data is suggestive
> rather than definitive but the consensus from major
> obstetric organizations is to **avoid licorice in
> pregnancy**. Includes licorice candy, licorice teas, and
> supplements. `established`

**The licorice-induced testosterone issue:**

> A small clinical study (Armanini 1999) found that men
> consuming the equivalent of 500 mg glycyrrhizin daily
> experienced ~26% drops in serum testosterone. Another study
> showed no significant effect. Practical relevance at tea
> doses is limited but worth knowing for men with low-T
> concerns. `attested`

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"All licorice candy contains licorice root"** — Most
>   "licorice" candy in North America actually uses anise oil,
>   not licorice root. Real-licorice products typically state
>   "made with licorice extract" or "contains glycyrrhizin."
>   The safety concerns apply only to real licorice products.
>   `verified`
> - **"Licorice cures cancer"** — Pre-clinical signals; no
>   clinical translation. `folk`
> - **"Licorice fixes adrenal fatigue"** — "Adrenal fatigue"
>   is not a recognized medical diagnosis; the underlying
>   mechanism (cortisol extension) is real but the framing is
>   problematic. `folk`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origin:**

> *Glycyrrhiza glabra* is native to southern Europe and parts
> of western Asia (Mediterranean basin, Iran, Iraq, Turkey,
> Caucasus). *G. uralensis* is native to Central Asia, China,
> and Mongolia. Both species have been cultivated for thousands
> of years; modern commercial production is in Italy, Spain,
> Greece, France, Iran, Iraq, Turkey, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan,
> Syria, Afghanistan, India, China, the United States, and
> England. The plant is among the most commercially valuable
> medicinal plants globally, with major uses spanning
> pharmaceuticals, tobacco (as a flavoring agent), cosmetics,
> confectionery, and food. `verified`

**Historical timeline — among the most-documented medicinal plants:**

> - **~3000 BCE:** Egyptian medical papyri document licorice
>   use. Tutankhamun's tomb contained licorice root supplies
>   (1323 BCE) — among the burial provisions for the pharaoh's
>   afterlife. `verified`
> - **~500 BCE:** Hippocrates references licorice. Greek and
>   Roman medicine adopt it for cough, ulcer, and respiratory
>   complaints. `verified`
> - **~300 BCE:** Theophrastus describes licorice. `verified`
> - **~70 CE:** Dioscorides documents licorice in *De Materia
>   Medica*. `verified`
> - **~200 CE (Han Dynasty China):** Licorice (*Gan Cao*)
>   becomes one of the foundational herbs of Traditional Chinese
>   Medicine. The *Shennong Bencao Jing* (神農本草經, the
>   foundational Chinese materia medica, ~200 CE) lists Gan
>   Cao as a "superior" herb. `verified`
> - **~25 CE onward:** Chinese physicians use licorice to
>   "tonify the heart and spleen's *qi*" and to harmonize
>   herbal formulas. The proverb "九方十甘草" ("nine of every
>   ten formulas use licorice") emerges, capturing licorice's
>   universal harmonizing role. `verified`
> - **Medieval Islamic Golden Age:** Avicenna's *Canon of
>   Medicine* (~1025 CE) includes licorice. Persian and Arab
>   medicine integrate licorice extensively. `verified`
> - **Medieval European herbalism:** Continuous use; licorice
>   becomes one of the major confectionery ingredients in
>   addition to medicinal use. `verified`
> - **17th-19th century:** Licorice candy and licorice
>   confectionery industries develop in Europe (especially
>   the Netherlands, Italy, England) and North America.
>   "Pontefract cakes" — pure licorice candy from Pontefract,
>   England — date from the 14th century onward. `verified`
> - **20th century:** Pharmacological characterization of
>   glycyrrhizin and the pseudoaldosteronism mechanism.
>   Discovery that licorice candy consumption could cause
>   medically-significant hypertension cases drove safety
>   research (1940s-1980s). `verified`
> - **1950s-1960s:** DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice)
>   developed and clinically validated for ulcer treatment
>   (Revers 1948 onward; Tewari 1973; Morgan 1985 onward).
>   `verified`
> - **2010s-2020s:** Continued widespread use globally; major
>   role in TCM, Ayurvedic, and Western herbal practice.
>   Antiviral interest grows during COVID pandemic; some
>   limited evidence for SARS-CoV-2 (mostly in vitro).
>   `verified`

**The Tutankhamun connection:**

> Real licorice root supplies were found in Tutankhamun's tomb
> (entombed ~1323 BCE), provisioning the young pharaoh for
> the afterlife. This is one of the most direct archaeological
> evidences for ancient Egyptian licorice use and gives
> licorice a documented medical-cultural history of at least
> 3,300 years. `verified`

**TCM framing — the universal harmonizer:**

> Licorice's role in TCM is uniquely central. Some key points:
>
> - **Classification:** Sweet (*gan*), neutral; enters all 12
>   meridians (one of very few herbs with this universal
>   character)
> - **Functions in TCM:**
>   - *Bu pi yi qi* (補脾益氣): Tonifies spleen, augments qi
>   - *Run fei zhi ke* (潤肺止咳): Moistens lungs, stops cough
>   - *Huan ji zhi tong* (緩急止痛): Eases urgency, stops pain
>   - *Tiao he zhu yao* (調和諸藥): **Harmonizes all herbs** —
>     the most distinctive role
>   - *Qing re jie du* (清熱解毒): Clears heat, resolves toxins
>   (raw form especially)
> - **The harmonizing role:** Licorice is included in formulas
>   for three main reasons: (1) to moderate the harshness of
>   strong herbs, (2) to bring different herbs into
>   collaboration rather than conflict, (3) to disguise bitter
>   or acrid flavors of other components
> - **Traditional inclusion rate:** Estimated 9 of 10 classical
>   formulas contain licorice
>
> No other catalog ingredient has anything like this universal
> harmonizing role. Worth surfacing in user-facing copy as the
> defining cultural-pharmacological identity of licorice.
> `verified`

**Modern clinical evidence:**

> Licorice has accumulated substantial clinical research:
>
> - **Gastric ulcers (DGL):** Multiple RCTs from 1970s-1990s
>   show DGL effective for peptic ulcer healing, comparable
>   to cimetidine in some trials (Morgan 1985, Russell 1984,
>   Tewari 1973). Most-validated clinical application.
> - **Throat irritation/sore throat:** Multiple modern trials
>   show licorice gargle effective for post-operative sore
>   throat (especially after intubation).
> - **Cough/respiratory:** Traditional use; modern evidence
>   for some formulations. Glycyrrhizin antiviral activity
>   documented in vitro against multiple respiratory viruses.
> - **Hepatitis (especially Hepatitis B and C):** Glycyrrhizin
>   IV preparation (Stronger Neo-Minophagen C) used in Japan
>   for hepatitis treatment. Oral licorice less established
>   for this.
> - **Mucositis (chemotherapy/radiation-induced):** Licorice
>   muco-adhesive films show benefit (Pakravan 2021 RCT).
> - **Adrenal "support":** Traditional use for "adrenal
>   fatigue" framing; cortisol-extending mechanism is real
>   but the framing is problematic.
> - **Antiviral:** Strong in vitro evidence; clinical
>   translation primarily in Asian medicine traditions.
> - **Mechanism (well-characterized):**
>   - 11β-HSD2 inhibition (the cortisol-extension mechanism)
>   - Anti-inflammatory via cortisol-extending effect
>   - Antiviral (glycyrrhizin shows direct antiviral activity
>     against several DNA and RNA viruses including HSV)
>   - Demulcent (mucilaginous polysaccharides)
>   - Estrogenic and anti-estrogenic effects (complex
>     hormonal interactions)
>   - Selective inhibition of thrombin (platelet effects)
>
> The clinical evidence is strong for ulcer (DGL) and throat
> applications; moderate for several other indications;
> heterogeneous for many traditional uses. `established` (DGL
> for ulcer); `established` (mechanism); `attested` (most
> clinical applications).

**Ayurvedic framing — *Yashtimadhu*:**

> Sanskrit *Yashtimadhu* (*yashti* = stick, *madhu* = sweet);
> Hindi *Mulethi*. Classical Ayurvedic classification:
>
> - **Rasa (taste):** *Madhura* (sweet)
> - **Virya (potency):** *Sheeta* (cooling — note this differs
>   from TCM's neutral classification)
> - **Vipaka:** *Madhura* (sweet)
> - **Dosha effect:** Pacifies *Vata* and *Pitta*; can
>   aggravate *Kapha* in excess
> - **Classification:** *Rasayana* (rejuvenative) and *medhya*
>   (cognitive-supporting)
>
> Used for: sore throat, cough, hoarseness, ulcers, eye
> conditions, and as a *rasayana*. The cooling classification
> matches the Western "soothing" framing. `verified`

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **The safety profile is the most significant in the catalog.**
   Real, documented, dose-dependent risk requires honest and
   prominent disclosure. Different from valerian's sedation
   warning (functional/temporary impairment) — licorice's risk
   is cumulative and can damage cardiovascular health silently.
   UI/UX treatment should make this visible.

2. **Tea is a reasonable but suboptimal preparation.**
   Tea-strength delivers meaningful glycyrrhizin but blend-
   form (5-15% of a multi-herb preparation) is the
   traditional and safer form. User-facing copy should
   guide users toward blend rather than solo licorice tea.

3. **The pregnancy contraindication is firm.** Finnish cohort
   studies provide strong epidemiological evidence; the
   mechanism (glucocorticoid effects on fetal HPA development)
   is biologically plausible. Should be a prominent flag.

4. **The "harmonizer" role doesn't map to vocabulary effects.**
   Licorice's most distinctive contribution is how it modifies
   *other* herbs in a blend. This is a blend-recommendation
   property rather than a felt-effect; worth surfacing in
   blend-logic and in copy when licorice appears as a blend
   component.

5. **DGL alternative.** Worth mentioning in user-facing copy
   for users who want digestive/throat support without
   pseudoaldosteronism risk. DGL is mostly a supplement form
   outside catalog tea scope but is the safer alternative
   when chronic/daily licorice support is wanted.

6. **TCM raw vs. honey-fried distinction.** Real and
   pharmacologically distinct in TCM tradition; probably
   below the user-perception threshold for catalog splitting
   per Principle #18. Documented in research file as cultural
   depth.

7. **The candy confusion.** Most "licorice" candy in North
   America (especially red licorice) contains no real licorice
   root — just anise oil, sugar, and food coloring. Black
   licorice candy varies; some contains real licorice extract,
   some doesn't. The pseudoaldosteronism risk applies only to
   real-licorice products. Worth surfacing for users who think
   "I eat licorice candy already and I'm fine."

8. **Sweet axis ceiling at 5.** Licorice's intrinsic sweetness
   is the catalog ceiling for the sweet basic taste. Future
   ingredients are unlikely to exceed without artificial
   sweeteners (not on roadmap).

9. **The Tutankhamun depth.** Worth surfacing for cultural
   resonance — licorice has documented use back to ~3000 BCE,
   making it among the most historically-deep medicinal
   plants in any catalog.

10. **Pairing recommendations:**
    - **Marshmallow root** (additive demulcent throat
      soother)
    - **Slippery elm** (additive demulcent — not in catalog)
    - **Ginger** (warming + soothing synergy)
    - **Cinnamon** (sweet-spice harmony)
    - **Star anise** (anethole resonance)
    - **Fennel** (also anethole; sweet-anise reinforcement)
    - **Peppermint** (cooling balance to licorice's heaviness)
    - **In TCM formulas universally** as a 5-15% component
    These suggest licorice's role as flavor and effect modifier
    rather than primary ingredient.

## Addendum — `uplifting` kept, `grounding` removed (2026-08-02)

### `grounding` — removed

§5 rates six effects for licorice and `grounding` is not among them:
`soothing` 4 as the primary, `digestive` 2, `warming` 1, `calm` 1,
`uplifting` 1. The app shipped `grounding` 2 at the 98°C point anyway.

Being a root is not the same as being grounding. This catalogue's
grounding register is reishi's triterpenes, ashwagandha's
withanolides, aged pu-erh — deep, earthy, low-pitched. Licorice's
defining quality is the opposite pole of the flavour map: glycyrrhizin
is 30–50× the sweetness of sucrose, and §5 rates `sweet` at 5 as the
ingredient's defining felt quality. A cup that reads overwhelmingly
sweet is not what "settled, centering, earthy" describes.

### `uplifting` — kept, and weak on purpose

§5 already rates it 1, glossed "some users report mood-positive
effect." That is anecdote rather than evidence, and the rating's own
value says so — 1 is the floor of the scale.

There is a plausible mechanism rather than a proven one: glycyrrhizin
inhibits 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2, extending cortisol's
active life, which §5 already invokes for the anti-inflammatory and
`calm` 1 claims. A mild mood effect is consistent with that, and it is
the same mechanism behind licorice's well-documented blood-pressure
and potassium cautions — so it is not an inert ingredient.

**No trial tests licorice for mood.** Kept at 1, where it ships, as a
subjective-report claim on the same footing as the catalogue's other
traditional entries — and explicitly not as a clinical finding.
