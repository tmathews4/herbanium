## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> China's premier green tea — pan-fired to pressed flat spears,
> with a signature chestnut-and-fresh-bean sweetness that has
> earned it imperial tribute status for over 250 years.

**Short description:**

> Dragonwell is a pan-fired green tea from the West Lake (Xihu)
> region of Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China. The tea is named
> after Longjing ("Dragon Well") Village — where a mythic dragon
> supposedly inhabits a local well. Distinguished by its flat,
> pressed-spear leaf shape (from hand-firing in woks at precise
> temperatures) and its uniquely sweet, chestnut-like flavor
> profile. Granted imperial tribute tea status by Emperor Kangxi
> in the Qing Dynasty; designated 18 "imperial tribute" bushes
> at Shifeng by Emperor Qianlong in 1751 — bushes that are still
> alive and producing tea today. Protected by Chinese geographical
> indication; only tea grown in the specific 168-km² West Lake
> zone can legally carry the "Xihu Longjing" name.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- chestnut
- sweet
- nutty
- fresh
- bean

**Aroma notes:**

> The canonical Dragonwell aroma combines fresh-cut grass, toasted
> chestnut, fresh soybean ("bean-like" — a core tea-community
> descriptor), and a subtle floral top. Quality Dragonwell develops
> complexity across the aroma: the first impression is fresh
> spring green, the middle notes bring the characteristic chestnut-
> bean warmth, and the finish has a lingering sweet-floral quality
> often compared to honeysuckle or gardenia.

**Mouthfeel:**

> Silky, slightly viscous, with a characteristic "coating" effect
> on the tongue — more substantial than most green teas. The
> mouthfeel distinguishes authentic West Lake Longjing from cheaper
> Longjing-style teas grown elsewhere. Minimal astringency when
> brewed properly; a clean sweet finish with "returning sweetness"
> (*hui gan*) similar to but distinct from oolong's.

**Basic tastes:**

> - `bitter` (1) — notably low for a green tea. The pan-firing
>   process produces a softer, sweeter character than steam-
>   processed Japanese greens.
> - `sweet` (3) — genuine natural sweetness is a defining feature.
>   Different from gyokuro's theanine-sweet; more toasted-grain-
>   sweet from pan-firing.
> - `umami` (2) — present but less than Japanese shaded greens.

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [75, 85] | traditional, Chinese tea practice | 80-85°C is canonical; first infusion lower (~75°C) for premium Ming Qian Longjing to preserve delicate aromatics |
| **time range (seconds)** | [60, 120] | traditional | 1-2 min Western style; gongfu: 15-30s with multiple infusions. Extracts faster than rolled greens because leaves are flat-spread rather than tightly packed. |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 30 | Csupor 2016 extrapolation; Teasenz | Moderate for a green tea; roughly half the caffeine of black tea at the same weight. Lower than gunpowder. |
| **dose** | 1 tsp (~3g) per 200ml | traditional | Generous leaf loading works; tea is forgiving |

> **Glass cup tradition:** Dragonwell is traditionally brewed in a
> glass cup (not a teapot) in Chinese practice — so drinkers can
> watch the flat leaves dance as they settle in the water. The
> visual is part of the experience.

### 6d. Time-axis behavior

> Dragonwell is moderately forgiving — more so than gyokuro but
> less than gunpowder. The flat-spear leaf shape means leaves are
> not tightly packed (unlike rolled gunpowder or ball-rolled
> oolong), so extraction is quick and relatively even.
>
> Over-brewing (5+ minutes) pulls forward bitterness and loses the
> characteristic sweetness — the hallmark of Dragonwell quality.
> Multi-infusion brewing is the canonical Chinese approach:
> 3-5 infusions from one dose, each shorter at first then gradually
> extending.
>
> **Visual brewing (canonical Chinese method):** In a tall glass,
> pour hot water halfway, add 3-5g Dragonwell leaves, then top
> with more water. The leaves "dance" — standing upright then
> settling gradually — which is part of the cultural experience.
> Drink when the tea reaches roughly 1/3 remaining, then refill
> with hot water for the next infusion.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**Known interactions:**

> Standard green tea cautions — caffeine (~30mg/cup), iron
> absorption interference at meals, fluoride at very heavy
> consumption. No Dragonwell-specific concerns. `established`

**Dosage cautions:**

> 3-5 cups/day is well within the studied range for green tea.
> Like other green teas, high-dose EGCG extracts can be hepatotoxic
> in rare cases — not a concern at normal brewed-tea consumption.

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"All Dragonwell comes from West Lake"** — Most commercial
>   Dragonwell is produced outside the protected zone — in Yunnan,
>   Guizhou, Sichuan, Guangdong. Only teas from the specific
>   168-km² West Lake protected region can legally bear "Xihu
>   Longjing." The rest is "Dragonwell-style" tea at much lower
>   price points. Neither wrong nor fake — just differently
>   labeled. `established`
> - **"The 18 imperial bushes are just legend"** — The specific
>   18 tea bushes designated by Emperor Qianlong in 1751 at
>   Shifeng Mountain are still alive, identifiable, and producing
>   tea. They are a documented living heritage. `verified`
> - **"Longjing cures cancer"** — Common in wellness marketing.
>   EGCG in vitro studies don't translate to clinical cancer
>   effects at normal consumption. `folk`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origin and cultivars:**

> Dragonwell is primarily made from three cultivars of *Camellia
> sinensis var. sinensis*:
>
> - **Qunti (群体种)**: The traditional heirloom cultivar, seed-
>   grown, highly variable. Produces more complex, robust tea
>   with deeper flavor. Premium West Lake Longjing from old-
>   growth gardens (like the Weng family's Shifeng garden) uses
>   this cultivar.
> - **Longjing #43 (龙井43号)**: A commercial cultivar bred for
>   earlier sprouting and higher yield. Dominant in industrial
>   production. Produces consistent but less complex tea than
>   Qunti.
> - **Jiukeng (九坑)**: A lesser-known complex cultivar used in
>   some premium productions outside the core West Lake zone.
> `verified`

**Historical timeline — over 1,200 years:**

> - **Tang Dynasty (618-907 AD):** Lu Yu, author of the *Cha Jing*
>   (Classic of Tea, ~760 AD), notes tea from Tianzhu and Lingyin
>   temples in the Hangzhou region. Earliest verifiable reference
>   to what would become Longjing. `verified`
> - **~1000 AD (Song Dynasty):** Monk Bian Cai plants a tea
>   seedling at Shifeng Mountain near Longjing Temple — by
>   tradition, the ancestor of modern Longjing tea. `attested`
> - **Song Dynasty (960-1279):** Longjing rises to regional
>   prominence, associated with scholar-official culture in
>   Hangzhou. `verified`
> - **Ming Dynasty (1368-1644):** Longjing gains broader
>   recognition as a notable tea. Production techniques refined.
>   `verified`
> - **Qing Dynasty — the imperial era:**
>   - **Emperor Kangxi (reigned 1661-1722):** Grants Longjing
>     tribute tea status. `verified`
>   - **1751:** Emperor Qianlong visits Hangzhou, personally picks
>     tea buds at Shifeng Mountain. His mother recovers from
>     illness after drinking tea he brings back. Qianlong
>     designates 18 specific tea bushes at Shifeng as "Imperial
>     Tribute Tea" — those 18 bushes are still alive and producing
>     today, over 270 years later. `verified`
> - **1915:** Longjing wins gold medal at the Panama-Pacific
>   International Exposition in San Francisco, establishing
>   international reputation. `verified`
> - **2001:** Chinese government grants Xihu Longjing
>   Geographical Indication (GI) protection; 168-km² West Lake
>   zone legally defines the "authentic" product. `verified`
> - **2016:** Served at G20 Summit in Hangzhou to visiting heads
>   of state — cementing its status as China's premier
>   diplomatic tea. `verified`

**The five villages of West Lake:**

> Authentic Xihu Longjing is produced in five traditional villages
> within the protected West Lake zone:
>
> 1. **Shi Feng (Lion Peak / 狮峰)**: The most prestigious — the
>    Qianlong 18 imperial bushes are here. Mild foggy microclimate
>    on the high slopes; produces the deepest, most complex
>    Longjing.
> 2. **Longjing Village (龙井村)**: The historic namesake village.
>    The mythic "dragon well" is located here.
> 3. **Mei Jia Wu (梅家坞)**: Over 500 family farms; produces
>    vibrant jade-green Longjing.
> 4. **Yun Xi (云栖, "Cloud Dwelling")**: High-elevation, cooler
>    microclimate, slower-growing leaves with more flavor
>    development.
> 5. **Hu Pao (虎跑, "Tiger Run")**: Famous for its spring water
>    as well as its tea. Premium cups often use Hu Pao spring
>    water with Shi Feng leaves — a traditional pairing.
> `verified`

**Grades — by harvest timing:**

> Longjing is graded primarily by harvest timing, which correlates
> with leaf tenderness and flavor concentration:
>
> - **Ming Qian (明前, "pre-Qingming")**: Harvested before the
>   Qingming Festival (April 4-5). The most delicate, most
>   expensive grade. Typically just one bud + one leaf. Limited
>   annual production window (~2-3 weeks).
> - **Yu Qian (雨前, "pre-Grain Rain")**: Harvested after Qingming
>   but before Gu Yu (April 20). Still premium; more robust
>   character.
> - **Post-Guyu**: Mid-season grade; quality still good, price
>   much lower.
> - **Summer/autumn harvests**: Workhorse quality for commercial
>   production.
> `verified`

**Processing — the pan-firing craft:**

> Dragonwell's signature flat leaf shape comes from an intricate
> hand-firing process in large shallow woks over charcoal or gas
> heat. The tea master uses bare hands against the hot wok surface
> (traditionally around 150-180°C), pressing and shaping leaves
> through a sequence of 10+ distinct hand motions — each with a
> specific name in Chinese tea-making vocabulary.
>
> The process combines *shaqing* (kill-green — halting enzymatic
> oxidation) with simultaneous shaping. Master tea makers develop
> pan-firing skills over decades; the motion patterns are
> intuitive and difficult to mechanize. Even in modern production,
> the highest grades are still hand-fired; lower grades use
> machine-assisted processing.
>
> The Weng family of Shifeng has made traditional Longjing by hand
> for generations — a direct lineage of the Qing Dynasty imperial
> tribute production. Master Weng Shangyi made Shifeng Longjing
> until his death in 2019 at age 90; his apprentice Chen Xiaochao
> and daughter Weng Shunqin continue the tradition today.
> `verified`

**Cultural significance:**

> Longjing occupies a central place in Chinese tea culture:
>
> - **Imperial tribute tea** for centuries, still used as a state
>   gift at diplomatic events.
> - **#1 of China's "Top Ten Famous Teas"** in most traditional
>   rankings.
> - **The "scholar's tea"** — long associated with Chinese literary
>   and artistic traditions; featured in poetry, paintings, and
>   cultural ceremonies.
> - **Qingming Festival tradition**: Hangzhou locals visit family
>   tea gardens during Qingming (traditionally a grave-sweeping
>   holiday) to select the year's tea — a family ritual
>   intertwined with honoring ancestors.
> `verified`

**TCM framing:**

> Like all unoxidized green teas, Longjing is TCM-cooling (Yin).
> The pan-firing process (distinct from Japanese steaming) produces
> a slightly less cooling character than sencha — some tea
> traditions call it "neutral-cooling." Suitable for spring and
> summer, less for winter. Classical TCM literature attributes
> benefits including mental clarity and "cooling heat" —
> consistent with modern research on green tea polyphenols.
> `attested`

## 11. Research flags & open questions

1. **Authenticity versus "Longjing-style" products.** Most
   commercial Longjing is from outside the protected West Lake
   zone — produced in Yunnan, Guizhou, Sichuan, Guangdong using
   Longjing processing techniques but different terroir. These
   are legally "Longjing-style" rather than "Xihu Longjing."
   Herbanium's `dragonwell` entry implicitly means the style;
   premium-tier users need to know the authenticity distinction.

2. **Cultivar variation is significant.** Qunti (heirloom) vs.
   Longjing #43 (commercial) produce noticeably different teas
   from the same terroir. Qunti is increasingly rare as
   commercial gardens replace old bushes. Worth considering for
   future variant representation.

3. **Grade by harvest timing is crucial for quality.** Ming Qian
   (pre-Qingming) vs. later harvests represents a larger quality
   gap than is common in most tea categories. Current Herbanium
   data treats Dragonwell as one ingredient; worth considering
   harvest-timing variants.

4. **The 18 imperial bushes specifically produce tea sold at
   extraordinary prices** — often >$1000/oz for the few grams
   harvested from those specific plants annually. This is a
   collector/gift market beyond Herbanium's typical scope but
   worth noting culturally.

5. **Visual brewing experience is part of the product.** Glass-cup
   brewing — watching flat leaves dance and settle — is
   traditionally how Dragonwell is experienced. Loses this in
   standard teapot brewing; worth surfacing as a recommendation.

6. **Counterfeiting is a real issue.** Due to premium pricing,
   fake West Lake Longjing is common. Authenticity markers:
   flat-pressed spears (not rolled), light olive-green color
   (not dark green), characteristic sweet-bean aroma. Herbanium
   users buying premium Longjing should be aware.
