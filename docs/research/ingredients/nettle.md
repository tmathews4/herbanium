## 2. Overview

**One-line essence:**

> The "burning that heals" — a sharply stinging European weed
> that, once dried or cooked, becomes one of the most mineral-
> rich, gently-nourishing herbal teas in Western tradition,
> with documented clinical support for allergic rhinitis and
> a 2,500+ year continuous medicinal record from Hippocrates
> through medieval Europe to modern phytotherapy.

**Short description:**

> Stinging nettle is the dried leaves and aerial parts (and
> sometimes roots) of *Urtica dioica*, a perennial herbaceous
> plant native to Europe and Western Asia, now naturalized
> across temperate regions globally. The plant is famous for
> its **stinging hairs** (trichomes) covering leaves and stems
> — fragile silica-tipped hollow needles that break off on
> contact, injecting a cocktail of histamine, acetylcholine,
> serotonin, and formic acid into skin. The result is the
> characteristic burning-itching urticaria (the medical term
> "urticaria" itself derives from *Urtica*) lasting up to 24
> hours. **Processing destroys the stinging:** drying,
> freeze-drying, cooking, or even thorough mechanical
> processing breaks the hairs and dissipates their chemistry,
> leaving a safe and nourishing herb. Dried nettle has been
> used as food, animal feed, fiber (the Bronze Age yielded
> nettle-fiber textiles), and medicine for at least 3,000
> years. Hippocrates documented over 60 medicinal uses;
> Roman soldiers reportedly self-flagellated with nettle
> branches to stay warm in cold climates (*urtication*); medieval
> European herbalists used it as a "spring tonic" — eating
> first young nettle shoots as a nutrient-restoring food after
> winter scarcity. Modern research has characterized solid
> mechanism for allergic rhinitis support: nettle extracts
> inhibit histamine H1 receptor (acting as both antagonist and
> negative agonist), inhibit mast cell tryptase (preventing
> degranulation), and inhibit COX-1, COX-2, and HPGDS
> (prostaglandin pathways). The clinical evidence is moderate
> but consistent: Mittman 1990 RCT (98 subjects) showed
> freeze-dried nettle preferred over placebo for hay fever;
> 2017 Iranian RCT (74 subjects) showed reduced eosinophil
> counts in allergic rhinitis. Other documented uses include
> benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) symptom support
> (Safarinejad 2005), modest blood pressure lowering, and
> blood glucose support in diabetes (Kianbakht 2013).
> Nutritionally, nettle is unusually rich in iron, calcium,
> magnesium, potassium, vitamins A, C, K, and protein —
> earning its traditional "spring tonic" reputation honestly.

## 3. Sensory — flavor & aroma

**Primary flavor notes:**

- earthy
- grassy
- mineral
- spinach-like
- subtly sweet

**Aroma notes:**

> Nettle smells distinctly green-vegetable — closer to spinach,
> Swiss chard, or other leafy greens than to most tea herbs.
> Dried nettle has a hay-like quality with mineral undertones.
> Quality nettle smells fresh and grassy; stale nettle smells
> dull and dusty. The aromatic profile is genuinely "vegetal
> nourishment" — not floral, not spicy, not bitter, but distinctly
> "green leaf food."

**Mouthfeel:**

> Smooth and approachable — nettle has none of the stinging
> quality of the fresh plant. The cup feels substantial but
> not heavy; some natural sweetness from chlorophyll-related
> compounds; very mild astringency. Most users find nettle
> immediately pleasant in a way that surprises those expecting
> a "medicinal" taste. Compare to spinach or kale water —
> mineral, vegetal, gently nourishing.

**Basic tastes:**

> - `earthy` (3) — defining; the spinach/kale register
> - `mineral` (3) — distinctive; iron and other minerals
>   contribute to flavor
> - `umami` (1-2) — slight savory undertone
> - `bitter` (1) — minimal; mild
> - `sweet` (1) — natural mild sweetness
> - `astringent` (1) — modest

> **Sensory note:** Nettle is one of the catalog's most
> "food-like" tea ingredients. The flavor profile resembles
> drinking the cooking water from leafy greens. Pairs
> naturally with food — savory meals, vegetable broths, or
> as a daily drinking tea between meals. Not a dessert
> beverage; not a "treat" tea.

## 4. Brewing — baseline parameters

| Parameter | Value | Source | Notes |
|-----------|-------|--------|-------|
| **temp range (°C)** | [95, 100] | traditional European | Full or near-boil; aerial parts extract well at high temperatures |
| **time range (seconds)** | [300, 900] | traditional European | 5-15 min — meaningful nutrient extraction takes longer than typical tea steeping |
| **caffeine (mg per ~8oz cup)** | 0 | universal | Caffeine-free |
| **dose** | 1-2 tsp dried nettle per 250ml | traditional | Long infusions (overnight cold-brew) used in herbalism for maximum mineral extraction |

> **Long-infusion tradition (Susun Weed nourishing herbal infusion):**
>
> Western herbalist Susun Weed popularized "nourishing herbal
> infusions" — a preparation where dried nettle is infused in
> just-boiled water for 4-8+ hours (typically overnight) in a
> covered jar. This long extraction:
> - Maximizes mineral extraction (iron, calcium, magnesium)
> - Yields a deep green-brown decoction
> - Tastes more robust and "mineraly" than short steeps
> - Used as a daily drinking tonic, often quart-volume
>
> The long-infusion approach is more like decoction than
> steeping. Suitable for users seeking maximum nutritional
> support. Standard 5-15 minute steeps remain the more common
> approach.

> **Spring tonic timing:**
>
> Traditional European practice harvested young nettle shoots
> in early spring (March-April) as the first fresh greens after
> winter food scarcity. Eaten as cooked greens (similar to
> spinach), as soup base, or brewed as tea. The seasonal
> framing ("spring nettle tonic") connects nettle to renewal-
> after-winter cultural rhythms in European herbalism. Worth
> surfacing in user-facing copy for cultural depth.

## 7. Safety & heads-up

**Generally well-tolerated:**

> Dried/cooked/processed nettle has a clean safety profile.
> The plant has been used as food and medicine for thousands
> of years across European, Asian, and indigenous American
> cultures. Documented adverse effects from prepared nettle
> are uncommon and mild:
> - Occasional mild digestive upset
> - Rare allergic reactions (hypersensitivity)
> - Diuretic effect at high doses (real but mild)
>
> `established`

**The fresh-plant stinging warning:**

> **Fresh nettle leaves and stems sting on contact** through
> trichome injection of histamine, acetylcholine, serotonin,
> and formic acid (Oliver 1991 mechanism study). This is not
> a tea-preparation concern but worth noting for users handling
> fresh nettle for harvest or cooking. Effects:
> - Immediate burning, stinging sensation
> - Red welts (urticaria) developing within minutes
> - Itching, sometimes lasting up to 24 hours
> - Severe reactions rare but possible (one case report of
>   anaphylaxis — Morgan 2003)
>
> Processing destroys the stinging: drying, freeze-drying,
> blanching, cooking, thorough crushing. Processed nettle
> products are completely safe for contact and consumption.
> `established`

**Known interactions:**

> - **Anticoagulants (especially warfarin):** Nettle is rich
>   in **vitamin K** — significant levels can interfere with
>   warfarin dosing. Users on warfarin should maintain consistent
>   nettle intake or discuss with their physician. Same
>   concern applies as with leafy greens generally. `established`
> - **Diuretics:** Mild additive diuretic effect; theoretical
>   electrolyte concerns at high doses combined with
>   prescription diuretics. `attested`
> - **Blood pressure medications:** Modest additive
>   hypotensive effect possible; nettle has documented
>   modest BP-lowering activity. `attested`
> - **Diabetes medications:** Nettle leaf shows blood glucose
>   lowering in clinical trials (Kianbakht 2013); theoretical
>   additive hypoglycemia. Monitor blood glucose if combining
>   with insulin or sulfonylureas. `attested`
> - **Lithium:** Nettle's diuretic effect could theoretically
>   affect lithium clearance; consult physician. `folk`
> - **NSAIDs:** Some pre-clinical evidence of additive anti-
>   inflammatory effect; potentially beneficial but worth
>   awareness. `folk`

**Pregnancy:**

> Some traditional use of nettle in pregnancy as a tonic;
> however, modern guidance is mixed:
> - **Late-pregnancy/labor preparation:** Some midwifery
>   tradition supports nettle use in third trimester
> - **Conservative practice:** Avoid medicinal-strength
>   nettle (especially supplements) without provider
>   guidance
> - **Food-strength use:** Cooked nettle as food appears
>   safe based on traditional use
>
> The vitamin K content is the main pharmacological concern;
> not generally problematic in pregnancy at culinary doses.
> `attested`

**Iron absorption note (in opposite direction from most teas):**

> Unlike *Camellia sinensis* teas (which inhibit non-heme iron
> absorption), nettle is *itself* iron-rich and may support
> iron status — especially relevant for vegetarians, pregnant
> women, and iron-deficient individuals. The traditional
> "spring tonic for blood building" framing aligns with the
> mineral content. `attested`

**NOT a concern but sometimes claimed:**

> - **"Nettle cures all"** — Hippocrates documented 60+ uses;
>   modern evidence supports allergic rhinitis, BPH symptom
>   support, modest BP/glucose effects. Other traditional uses
>   have varying evidence. Standard pattern. `folk` for
>   panacea; `established` for specific indications
> - **"Nettle is dangerous"** — Confusion with the fresh-
>   plant stinging effect; processed nettle is safe for nearly
>   everyone. `folk`

## 8. History & cultural context

**Plant origin:**

> *Urtica dioica* is native to Europe, Western Asia, and
> Northern Africa; now naturalized worldwide in temperate
> regions including all of North America. Grows abundantly
> in disturbed nitrogen-rich soils — nettle patches often
> indicate former human habitation or disturbed ground.
> Self-propagating; considered an aggressive weed in many
> agricultural contexts; cultivated commercially for medicinal
> and food use in Europe. The plant grows 1-2m tall, with
> heart-shaped serrated leaves and small green flowers
> appearing May-September. `verified`

**Historical timeline — among the most-documented European medicinal plants:**

> - **Bronze Age (~3000-1000 BCE):** Nettle fiber used for
>   textiles in Europe; nettle-fiber cloth predates linen
>   in some archaeological contexts. The plant served as
>   food, medicine, and material simultaneously. `verified`
> - **~400 BCE:** Hippocrates documents over 60 medicinal
>   uses of nettle — among the most extensively-documented
>   plants in the *Hippocratic Corpus*. `verified`
> - **Roman period (~50 BCE - 400 CE):** *Urtication* —
>   self-flagellation with fresh nettle branches — used by
>   Roman soldiers reportedly to stay warm in cold British
>   climates and for arthritis treatment. Pliny the Elder
>   and Dioscorides both document medicinal uses. `verified`
> - **Anglo-Saxon/Medieval Europe:** Nettle becomes a major
>   spring-tonic food after winter scarcity. Documented in
>   Anglo-Saxon medical texts (the *Lacnunga* and *Bald's
>   Leechbook*) and in nine-herbs charm. `verified`
> - **Medieval European herbalism:** Continuous documentation
>   through Hildegard of Bingen (~1150 CE), through the
>   herbal tradition of Gerard (1597), Culpeper (1653),
>   and others. Used for arthritis (urtication), allergies,
>   skin conditions, urinary issues, and as a spring tonic.
>   `verified`
> - **WWII Britain:** Nettle officially recommended as a
>   wartime food substitute (substitute for spinach) due to
>   high nutritional value and abundant availability. Also
>   used for fiber (cloth, paper) and dye. `verified`
> - **20th-21st century:** Modern phytotherapy continues
>   documentation. German Commission E approves nettle for
>   urinary tract support and BPH symptom support (1980s).
>   Mittman 1990 RCT establishes hay fever evidence. Multiple
>   subsequent trials support BPH, allergic rhinitis, and
>   metabolic indications. `verified`

**Cultural significance:**

> Nettle holds a distinctive cultural position in European
> tradition:
>
> - **Folklore:** Nettle appears in fairy tales (Hans
>   Christian Andersen's "The Wild Swans" — princess weaves
>   nettle shirts to free her brothers from enchantment) and
>   folk magic (nettle as protective herb, used in protection
>   bundles)
> - **The "burning that heals" archetype:** The sting that
>   produces the medicine — nettle is the prototypical example
>   of bitter-medicine paradox in European folk wisdom. The
>   urtication tradition (deliberately stinging for arthritis
>   relief — paradoxically, the stinging cocktail of histamine
>   and acetylcholine produces counter-irritation that some
>   users find genuinely relieves chronic pain)
> - **Spring tonic tradition:** Many European cultures have
>   "first nettle" rituals — the first young shoots gathered
>   in early spring, eaten as restorative food after winter.
>   This connects nettle to renewal, vitality, and seasonal
>   transition
> - **Shakespeare's reference:** *Henry IV Part 1* — "Out of
>   this nettle, danger, we pluck this flower, safety" —
>   capturing the cultural understanding that nettle's
>   challenging surface delivers genuine benefit
> - **Pet hate, hidden friend:** Modern gardeners often dread
>   nettle patches; herbalists treasure them. The cultural
>   ambivalence reflects the plant's dual nature
>
> User-facing copy can engage this depth honestly. `verified`

**Modern clinical evidence:**

> Nettle has accumulated solid clinical research across
> multiple indications:
>
> **Allergic rhinitis (hay fever):**
> - Mittman 1990: Randomized double-blind trial with 98
>   subjects; freeze-dried nettle preferred over placebo.
>   Foundational hay fever evidence.
> - 2017 Iranian RCT: 74 subjects with allergic rhinitis;
>   1 month of nettle leaf extract showed significantly reduced
>   eosinophil count and symptom improvement. PMC5963652
> - **Mechanism (well-characterized):** Roschek 2009 in vitro
>   study showed nettle extract inhibits H1 receptor (both
>   antagonist and negative agonist activity), inhibits mast
>   cell tryptase, inhibits COX-1/COX-2/HPGDS — multiple
>   complementary mechanisms in a single plant
>
> **Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH):**
> - Safarinejad 2005: 558 men with BPH; nettle root extract
>   showed 81% symptom improvement vs 16% placebo; improved
>   urinary peak flow and reduced post-void residual volume
> - Multiple combination-trial evidence (nettle + saw palmetto)
> - Approved by German Commission E for urinary tract issues
>
> **Diabetes:**
> - Kianbakht 2013: 92 type 2 diabetes patients; 500mg nettle
>   leaf extract 3x daily for 3 months; significantly reduced
>   fasting glucose, postprandial glucose, and HbA1c
>
> **Osteoarthritis:**
> - Some clinical evidence for nettle in pain reduction
>   (urtication tradition has some empirical support)
>
> **Mechanism summary:**
> - **Anti-allergic:** H1 receptor antagonism + mast cell
>   tryptase inhibition + COX inhibition (multiple complementary)
> - **Anti-inflammatory:** Multiple pathways (COX, leukotriene)
> - **Diuretic:** Mild but real
> - **Hypotensive:** Modest
> - **Hypoglycemic:** Modest
> - **Anti-androgenic (BPH):** 5α-reductase modulation
> - **Antioxidant:** Multiple compound classes
> - **Nutritional:** Iron, calcium, magnesium, vitamins A, C, K
>
> Net assessment: One of the better-evidenced traditional
> herbals; mechanism well-characterized for multiple
> indications. `established` (allergic rhinitis mechanism, BPH);
> `attested` (most other indications).

**Ayurvedic and other tradition framing:**

> Used in Ayurvedic medicine (*Vrishchhiyaa-shaaka*) for similar
> indications: skin conditions, urinary issues, joint pain.
> Used in Unani medicine (*Anjuraa*). Indigenous American
> peoples used New World nettle species similarly for arthritis,
> wound dressing, and as food. The cross-cultural convergence
> for similar uses (joint pain, allergies, urinary support,
> spring food) supports the genuine functional value. `verified`
